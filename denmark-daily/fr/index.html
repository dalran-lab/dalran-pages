The Open Sandwich — Smørrebrød and the Rye Beneath It

If noma is Denmark's culinary showpiece, smørrebrød is its everyday soul. The word means, simply, "butter-and-bread," but a proper smørrebrød is a small, composed open-faced sandwich — a slice of dense dark rye, buttered, then carefully built up with toppings into something closer to a plated dish than a snack.

Everything rests on the bread. Rugbrød — Danish rye — is a dark, dense, faintly sour sourdough loaf, packed with whole grains and seeds, and utterly central to the Danish diet. It fills the schoolchild's madpakke (packed lunch) and forms the foundation of every smørrebrød. On top go the classics: pickled herring (sild), liver pâté (leverpostej) with bacon and mushrooms, roast beef with crispy fried onions and remoulade, breaded plaice with lemon and remoulade, tiny shrimp heaped over egg, or the wonderfully named dyrlægens natmad — the "vet's midnight snack."

There is quiet etiquette to it. Smørrebrød is eaten with knife and fork, never overstacked, and worked through in a traditional order — herring first, then other fish, then meat, then cheese — often at a long, convivial lunch (frokost) lubricated with cold beer and shots of snaps (aquavit). The festive extreme is the julefrokost, the marathon Christmas lunch.

Like much of Danish food culture, smørrebrød has swung from humble to haute and back. Century-old institutions such as Ida Davidsen and Schønnemann in Copenhagen turned it into an art of dozens of varieties, and the New Nordic movement re-elevated it for a new generation. But its genius is that it never really left everyday life: on any given weekday, a Dane is unwrapping a slice of rye with something good on top — the most ordinary and most national of meals.
