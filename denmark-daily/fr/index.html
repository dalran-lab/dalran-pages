A Compass Needle Twitches — Hans Christian Ørsted and the Birth of Electromagnetism

In the spring of 1820, during a lecture at the University of Copenhagen, Hans Christian Ørsted noticed something no one had properly explained before: when he passed an electric current through a wire lying near a compass, the needle swung away from magnetic north. Electricity and magnetism, long treated as separate forces, were connected. Ørsted, born in 1777 in the small town of Rudkøbing on the island of Langeland, the son of a pharmacist, had spent two decades absorbed in chemistry, physics, and the philosophy of nature before this moment made him famous across Europe.

He did not rush into print. Ørsted spent months repeating and refining the experiment before publishing his findings on 21 July 1820 in a four-page Latin pamphlet, Experimenta circa effectum conflictus electrici in acum magneticam — "Experiments on the effect of an electric conflict on the magnetic needle." He mailed it directly to leading scientific societies rather than waiting for a journal, aware of the discovery's significance. Within weeks, André-Marie Ampère in Paris had worked out the mathematics of the relationship, and the new field of electromagnetism was underway. A generation later it fed directly into Michael Faraday's work on induction and, eventually, James Clerk Maxwell's unification of electricity, magnetism, and light — a chain of reasoning that runs, in a straight line, from a wobbling compass needle in a Copenhagen lecture hall to the electric motors and generators of the modern world.

Ørsted's curiosity ranged far beyond that single discovery. He was among the first, in 1825, to isolate metallic aluminium, and he later identified the alkaloid piperine, which gives black pepper its bite. Deeply committed to applied science, he helped found what became the Technical University of Denmark, DTU, in 1829, insisting that Danish industry and education needed a rigorous scientific foundation. He died in Copenhagen in 1851, having become one of the most honored scientists of his age, a friend to Hans Christian Andersen and a fixture of the city's intellectual life.

His legacy is written into everyday Danish life more than most visitors realize. The public garden Ørstedsparken, near Copenhagen's Nørreport station, bears his name, as does the CGS unit of magnetic field strength, the oersted. Most visibly, Denmark's dominant energy company renamed itself Ørsted in 2017 when it pivoted from fossil fuels to offshore wind — a fitting tribute to the man who first showed that electricity could move the invisible forces of the world.
