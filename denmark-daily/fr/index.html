Out of Africa and Back to Rungstedlund — Karen Blixen

Denmark's other great storyteller — after H.C. Andersen — lived a life as dramatic as any of her tales. Karen Blixen (1885–1962), who wrote in English under the name Isak Dinesen, was born at Rungstedlund, a manor on the coast north of Copenhagen that would bookend her life.

In 1914 she moved to British East Africa (today Kenya) to run a coffee farm with her husband, Baron Bror Blixen. The marriage failed, but Africa did not let go of her: she fell in love with the English aristocrat and big-game hunter Denys Finch Hatton, learned the land and its people, and poured seventeen years into a farm that ultimately went bankrupt. When it collapsed — and Finch Hatton died in a plane crash — she returned, in 1931, to Rungstedlund, middle-aged and without a career.

There she reinvented herself as a writer. Her "Seven Gothic Tales" (1934), written in English, made her a sensation in America. Then came her masterpiece, the memoir "Out of Africa" (1937), which opens with one of literature's most famous lines — "I had a farm in Africa, at the foot of the Ngong Hills" — and turned her lost farm into myth. More stories followed, among them "Babette's Feast," later filmed and, in 1987, winning Denmark its first Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film.

Blixen cultivated a theatrical, aristocratic persona and was several times mentioned as a Nobel contender; Hemingway, on winning his own, said she deserved it more. She died at Rungstedlund in 1962 and is buried in its grounds beneath a beech tree. The estate is now the Karen Blixen Museum, an easy trip up the coast from Copenhagen — and a reminder that one of the 20th century's great English-language storytellers was, at heart, a Danish baroness who had lost everything and written her way back.
