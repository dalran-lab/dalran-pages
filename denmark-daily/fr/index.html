The Builder King — Christian IV and the Making of Copenhagen

For sixty years — the longest reign in Danish history — one restless, hard-living, endlessly ambitious king reshaped his country's capital. Christian IV (1577–1648), who ruled from 1588 to 1648, is the reason Copenhagen looks the way it does.

A true Renaissance prince, Christian was obsessed with building, and much of it still stands. He raised Rosenborg Castle, the fairy-tale palace that now guards the Danish crown jewels. He built the Round Tower (Rundetårn), with its unique spiral ramp wide enough to ride a horse up, as an astronomical observatory. He gave the city the old stock exchange, Børsen, crowned by a spire of four intertwined dragon tails; the naval barracks of Nyboder; and the planned canal district of Christianshavn. He even founded cities abroad, rebuilding Oslo after a fire and renaming it Christiania in his own honour.

He was as colourful as his buildings — fathering more than twenty children, throwing himself into naval battle, and, according to legend, having the shrapnel that cost him an eye at the 1644 sea-fight of Colberger Heide made into earrings.

But ambition abroad proved ruinous. Christian dragged Denmark into the Thirty Years' War and lost badly (1625–1629), then into a disastrous war with Sweden. His reign, so glorious in stone, marked the start of Denmark's long decline against its Swedish rival — a reversal that would shape the next two centuries.

His legacy is stitched into modern Copenhagen: Rosenborg, the Round Tower and Nyboder are among its most visited sights. A recent tragedy underlined how alive his architecture remains — in April 2024 a fire tore through Børsen, collapsing its iconic dragon-spire and shocking the nation, which has vowed to rebuild it. Four centuries on, Christian IV's Copenhagen is still, unmistakably, the city he built.
