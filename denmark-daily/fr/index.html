The Boats to Sweden — The Rescue of the Danish Jews, 1943

Among the darkest years of the 20th century, Denmark holds one of its brightest stories — and it turns on ordinary people and small fishing boats.

Germany occupied Denmark in April 1940 but, at first, ruled with a light touch, leaving the Danish government and its small Jewish community of around 7,000–8,000 largely undisturbed. That changed in the summer of 1943, as resistance sabotage grew. In August the Danish government resigned rather than meet German demands for the death penalty against saboteurs, and the occupier imposed direct military rule. The Jews of Denmark were now in danger.

In late September 1943 the Germans set a date — the nights around 1–2 October, during the Jewish New Year — to round up and deport them. But the plan leaked. A German diplomat, Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz, secretly warned Danish contacts, who spread the alarm through synagogues and networks across the country. What followed was extraordinary: over about three weeks, ordinary Danes — doctors, fishermen, priests, students, police — hid their Jewish neighbours and then ferried more than 7,000 people across the narrow Øresund strait to neutral Sweden in fishing boats, night after night.

The result was almost unique in occupied Europe: roughly 95% of Denmark's Jews survived the war. Around 470 were caught and deported to the Theresienstadt ghetto, but Danish authorities relentlessly demanded access to them and sent aid parcels, and most of those, too, survived.

One footnote deserves correcting: the beloved tale that King Christian X wore a yellow star in solidarity is a myth — Danish Jews were never forced to wear one. The truth needs no embellishment. The rescue became, and remains, a cornerstone of how Denmark sees itself: a society whose instinct, tested at the worst possible moment, was collective solidarity.
