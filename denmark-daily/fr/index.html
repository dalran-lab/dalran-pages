Bigger Than It Looks — Greenland, the Faroes and the Danish Realm

On a map of Europe, Denmark is a small green scatter of islands. On a map of the world, it is one of the largest territorial states in the Western Hemisphere — because "Denmark" is really shorthand for the Kingdom of Denmark, a union that stretches deep into the North Atlantic and the Arctic.

This union is called the Danish Realm (Rigsfællesskabet, "community of the realm"). It joins Denmark proper to two self-governing territories: the Faroe Islands and Greenland. Both were once colonies; both are now autonomous nations that share the Danish crown, send two members each to the Folketing in Copenhagen, and leave foreign affairs and defence to Denmark — while running most of their own affairs.

The Faroe Islands (Føroyar) are 18 wind-scoured volcanic islands halfway to Iceland, home to about 54,000 people with their own language, parliament and thousand-year history. They won Home Rule in 1948 and have long guarded their distinctiveness — including staying outside the European Union even though Denmark joined.

Greenland (Kalaallit Nunaat) is the giant of the family: the world's largest island, roughly 50 times the size of Denmark proper, though almost entirely covered by ice and home to only about 57,000 people, mostly Inuit. It gained Home Rule in 1979 and far broader Self-Government in 2009, which explicitly opens a path to full independence should Greenlanders choose it. Its strategic weight is enormous — Arctic sea routes, rare minerals, and a major U.S. air base — which is why Greenland periodically lands on the world's front pages.

For a newcomer, the Realm is the key to a puzzle: how a gentle, consensus-minded country of under six million is also an Arctic power, wrestling with questions of independence, climate change and great-power attention far from the cosy cafés of Copenhagen.
