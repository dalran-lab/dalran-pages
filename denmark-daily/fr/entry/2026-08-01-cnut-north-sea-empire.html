The Dane Who Ruled England — Cnut and the North Sea Empire

A thousand years ago, the king of England was a Dane — and for a brief, dazzling moment, Denmark sat at the centre of a North Sea empire.

The story begins with Sweyn Forkbeard (Svend Tveskæg), son of Harald Bluetooth. A relentless raider of England, Sweyn finally conquered the whole kingdom in 1013, driving out King Æthelred the Unready — only to die a few weeks later, in early 1014. His work was completed by his son, Cnut the Great (Knud den Store, c. 995–1035), who secured the English throne in 1016 after hard campaigning.

Cnut then inherited Denmark (1018) and conquered Norway (1028), assembling a realm around the North Sea that historians call the North Sea Empire. Far from a plundering warlord, he ruled England as a wise and Christian king: he kept English law, endowed churches, married Æthelred's widow Emma to bind the two peoples, and in 1027 travelled to Rome for the imperial coronation of Conrad II, a Danish king moving as an equal among Europe's rulers.

He is best remembered, unfairly, for a story usually told upside down. According to legend, Cnut set his throne by the sea and commanded the tide to halt — not out of arrogance, but to prove to his flattering courtiers that even a king cannot hold back the waves, and that only God's power is limitless. It was a lesson in humility, not vanity.

The empire did not outlive its founder. After Cnut's death in 1035 it fragmented among his sons, and English rule returned to a native line in 1042. Yet the Danish imprint on England endured — in the Danelaw region of the north and east, in place-names ending in -by and -thorpe, and in everyday English words borrowed from Old Norse: sky, egg, knife, even they and them.
