The Atom and the Institute — Niels Bohr

No Dane has shaped modern science more than Niels Bohr (1885–1962). Born in Copenhagen, he gave physics its first workable picture of the atom and then built the institution where much of quantum mechanics was born.

In 1913, Bohr proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus only in fixed, quantised energy levels, jumping between them by absorbing or emitting precise packets of light. The Bohr model explained the spectrum of hydrogen and won him the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1922. It was one of the founding acts of quantum theory.

But his deepest influence was as a gatherer of minds. In 1921 he founded the Institute for Theoretical Physics in Copenhagen — now the Niels Bohr Institute — which became the world's magnetic centre for young physicists. There, with Werner Heisenberg and others, he developed the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics, the idea that a particle has no definite properties until it is measured. His long, courteous but profound debates with Albert Einstein, who insisted "God does not play dice," are among the most famous arguments in the history of science.

Bohr's life also ran straight through the century's darkest chapter. Of partly Jewish descent, he fled occupied Denmark in 1943, escaping by boat to Sweden and then to Britain and the United States, where he contributed to the Manhattan Project while arguing, presciently, for postwar openness and arms control. A wartime visit from Heisenberg in 1941 — its purpose still debated by historians — later inspired Michael Frayn's celebrated play Copenhagen.

His scientific dynasty endured: his son Aage Bohr won his own Nobel Prize in Physics in 1975. The element bohrium bears the family name, and the Niels Bohr Institute remains a world leader — a reminder that Denmark's outsized scientific reputation rests on the shoulders of one endlessly curious Copenhagener.
