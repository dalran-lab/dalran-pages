Yeast Under His Hat — J.C. Jacobsen and the Making of Carlsberg

Jacob Christian Jacobsen (1811–1887) grew up over a brewery. His father, a Jutland-born brewer, worked in Copenhagen, and the son inherited the trade along with a conviction that Denmark's warm, cloudy top-fermented beer could be bettered. The model was Bavaria, where brewers made clear, stable, bottom-fermented lager in cold cellars. On visits to Munich in the 1840s, Jacobsen obtained live lager yeast from Gabriel Sedlmayr of the Spaten brewery and carried it home by stagecoach in tin canisters, cooling them with water at every stop. The story that he kept the yeast under his top hat is probably embellished, but the science was real.

The first brew ran on 10 November 1847 at a new brewery on a hill in Valby, west of Copenhagen. Jacobsen named it Carlsberg after his son Carl and the hill (bjerg). He treated brewing as applied research, and in 1875 founded the Carlsberg Laboratory, one of the world's first serious industrial research institutions. There Emil Christian Hansen in 1883 isolated a single pure yeast cell and worked out how to propagate disease-free pitching yeast, publishing the method freely rather than patenting it; the strain became known as Saccharomyces carlsbergensis. The same laboratory gave the world the Kjeldahl method for measuring nitrogen (Johan Kjeldahl, 1883) and the pH scale (S.P.L. Sørensen, 1909).

In 1876 Jacobsen created the Carlsberg Foundation, leaving the brewery to it so that profits would fund Danish science in perpetuity, an arrangement that still holds. The Foundation also runs the Museum of National History at Frederiksborg Castle, which Jacobsen paid to rebuild after the 1859 fire. His relationship with Carl was stormy: the son opened a rival brewery, Ny Carlsberg, next door in 1882, and the two firms merged only in 1906. Carl's own philanthropy produced the Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek (1897) and the commission for The Little Mermaid (1913).

Carlsberg absorbed Tuborg in 1970 and is now among the largest brewers on earth. Beer production left Valby in 2008 for Fredericia, and the old site is being rebuilt as Carlsberg Byen, a dense new quarter around its own train station, the ornate 1901 Elephant Gate still standing guard.
