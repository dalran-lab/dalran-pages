Thirteen Studs a Second — How a Carpenter's Workshop Became LEGO

In 1932, in the small Jutland town of Billund, a carpenter named Ole Kirk Christiansen was struggling. His workshop built stepladders, ironing boards, and wooden furniture for local farmers, but the Depression had gutted demand. To keep his business alive, he began making wooden toys, small trucks, pull-along ducks, that he could sell more easily than furniture. Two years later he named the company LEGO, a contraction of the Danish phrase leg godt, meaning "play well." Only decades afterward did anyone point out that lego also means "I put together" in Latin, a coincidence the company has happily embraced ever since.

The leap that made LEGO recognizable worldwide came in 1949, when the firm, by then run alongside Ole Kirk's son Godtfred Kirk Christiansen, began producing interlocking plastic bricks inspired by a British design called Kiddicraft. The early bricks were serviceable but flawed: they didn't grip each other firmly enough to build anything tall or stable. Godtfred spent years refining the underside of the brick, adding hollow tubes that locked against the studs on top. On 28 January 1958, the company filed the patent for this stud-and-tube coupling system, the same basic geometry still used in every LEGO brick sold today. That single mechanical idea, simple, dry, and almost invisible to a child playing with it, is why a brick made in 1958 still clicks precisely onto one made last year.

The company's fortunes were nearly undone by fire twice, once in 1924 to the original workshop and again in 1960 to the wooden-toy warehouse, a loss that pushed the family to commit entirely to plastic. Billund itself grew up around the factory: when LEGO opened an airport there in 1964 to fly in parts and buyers, a town of a few hundred people suddenly had international air links, an odd inversion of how most Danish infrastructure developed from cities outward rather than from a toy factory outward.

Today LEGO remains privately held by the Kirk Christiansen family through the holding company KIRKBI, and Billund still houses both the original Lego House museum and the company's headquarters. For a country that exports butter, insulin, and wind turbines, Denmark's most recognized design object worldwide may well be a 1.6-centimeter plastic stud that a Jutland carpenter's son patented to stop a toy truck from falling apart.
