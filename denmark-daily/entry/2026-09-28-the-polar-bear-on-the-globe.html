The Polar Bear on the Globe — Nordisk Film and Denmark's Lost Movie Empire

On 2 November 1906, a former circus manager and fairground entrepreneur named Ole Olsen founded Nordisk Films Kompagni in Copenhagen, a company that today claims the title of the world's second-oldest continuously operating film studio, after France's Gaumont. Olsen had run cinemas and amusement parks before he saw money in making pictures rather than just screening them. He built a modest glass-roofed studio in the Copenhagen suburb of Valby, an area still known today as Filmbyen, "Film City," where Nordisk still produces work more than a century later.

Olsen's early business instincts were shrewd and occasionally lurid. He specialized in sensational melodramas, exotic travelogues, and staged "wild animal" hunting films that dramatized lion and tiger encounters, material that traveled well across language barriers in the silent era and sold briskly abroad. By the years just before the First World War, Nordisk had become one of the largest film producers in Europe, exporting Danish pictures to markets as far as Russia and South America and briefly making tiny Denmark a genuine power in world cinema, a status it would not hold again once the war disrupted export markets and Hollywood's studio system pulled ahead.

The company's trademark, a polar bear standing on a globe, became one of the most recognizable logos in early European film and is still used by Nordisk Film today. The studio survived the collapse of the Danish silent-film boom, weathered the German occupation years, and was eventually absorbed into the media group Egmont, under which it now operates cinemas across Scandinavia through Nordisk Film Biografer and funds new Nordic productions through the Nordisk Film & TV Fond.

Ole Olsen died in 1943, having already sold his stake decades earlier, but his gamble that moving pictures could be manufactured, exported, and branded like any other Danish industrial product proved prescient. Modern Danish cinema, from Dreyer's austere silent classics to today's internationally distributed television dramas, traces its industrial roots to the same Valby backlot where Olsen once filmed a caged lion for the camera.
