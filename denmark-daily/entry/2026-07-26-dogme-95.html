A Vow of Chastity — Dogme 95

In 1995 two Danish directors declared war on Hollywood — and accidentally launched one of the most influential film movements of the era. Lars von Trier and Thomas Vinterberg, working in Copenhagen, wrote a manifesto and a set of rules they called the Vow of Chastity (Kyskhedsløfter), and named the whole project Dogme 95.

The rules were deliberately austere, a rebellion against expensive spectacle and digital trickery. Films had to be shot on location with no imported props or sets; the camera had to be handheld; only natural light and live, on-set sound were allowed (no added soundtrack); genre films and special effects were forbidden; and — a very Danish touch — the director was not to be credited. Stripped of gloss, the film-maker would have to fall back on story and performance.

The results were startling. Vinterberg's "Festen" (The Celebration, 1998), a raw family drama shot on a small digital camera, won the Jury Prize at Cannes and remains the movement's masterpiece. Von Trier's provocative "Idioterne" (The Idiots, 1998) followed. Official "Dogme" certificates were issued to compliant films, and directors around the world took up the challenge.

The movement itself burned out within a few years — even its founders soon broke the rules — but its influence was enormous, helping to legitimise low-budget, handheld, digital film-making everywhere. And it announced a Danish cinema that has punched astonishingly above its weight ever since. Von Trier went on to win the Palme d'Or for Dancer in the Dark (2000) and co-founded the studio Zentropa; Vinterberg won the Academy Award for Best International Feature for Another Round (Druk, 2020); and Danish talents from director Nicolas Winding Refn to actor Mads Mikkelsen became global names. For a country of under six million, it is a remarkable run — and it began with a vow to give things up.
