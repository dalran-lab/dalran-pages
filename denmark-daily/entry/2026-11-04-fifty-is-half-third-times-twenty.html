Fifty Is "Half-Third Times Twenty" — Denmark's Vigesimal Numbers

Ask a Dane what halvtreds means and most will say "fifty" without a flicker of hesitation. Ask them why, and fewer can answer. The word is a contraction of halvtredsindstyve, literally "half-third times twenty": two full twenties plus a half-twenty on the way to a third, which is 2.5 × 20 = 50. The same logic runs through tres (tresindstyve, "three times twenty" = 60), halvfjerds (halvfjerdsindstyve, "half-fourth times twenty" = 70), firs (firsindstyve, "four times twenty" = 80), and halvfems (halvfemsindstyve, "half-fifth times twenty" = 90). Danish, alone among the mainland Scandinavian languages, builds its higher numbers on twenty rather than ten.

This vigesimal counting is not a Danish invention. Counting in scores shows up across old Germanic and Celtic speech, surviving today in French quatre-vingts ("four twenties," 80) and in the biblical English phrase "threescore and ten." It likely reflects an older, pre-decimal way of tallying goods, livestock, or coins in bundles of twenty, the number of fingers and toes a person has to count on. Swedish and Norwegian eventually regularized their number words onto a purely base-ten pattern, so a Swede says femtio, sextio, sjuttio for 50, 60, 70 with no mental arithmetic required. Danish never made that switch in everyday speech, and the old forms simply calcified into fixed vocabulary.

Crucially, modern Danes are not doing fractions in their heads every time they order a kilo of rugbrød for halvtreds kroner. The etymology has gone opaque, the way "breakfast" no longer evokes breaking a fast. Children learn halvtreds, tres, halvfjerds, firs, halvfems as arbitrary labels, the same way English speakers memorize "eleven" without parsing it as "one left" over ten.

For foreigners settling in Copenhagen, these numbers are a notorious early obstacle, since telephone numbers, prices, and addresses all run through them constantly. The Dansk Sprognævn, the state body that governs Danish spelling and usage, treats the vigesimal forms as entirely standard; no reform has ever been seriously proposed. It remains one of the clearest linguistic fingerprints separating Danish from its Nordic cousins, a small daily reminder that languages carry fossils of how people once counted the world.
