Flesh That Outlasted Two Thousand Years — The Tollund Man and Jutland's Bog Bodies

On 6 May 1950, two brothers cutting peat near the village of Tollund, in the Bjældskovdal bog west of Silkeborg, uncovered a face so fresh they first mistook it for a recent murder victim and called the police. What they had found was a man who had died roughly 2,400 years earlier, during Denmark's Pre-Roman Iron Age, around 400–380 BC. The acidic, oxygen-starved peat of Jutland's bogs had tanned his skin like leather while slowly dissolving his skeleton, leaving a head and feet so intact that the stubble on his chin and the creases of his eyelids are still visible. A leather cap sat on his head, and a plaited leather rope was drawn tight around his neck: the Tollund Man had been hanged, his body then laid gently in the bog rather than discarded, legs drawn up as if sleeping.

The archaeologist P.V. Glob, who directed the excavation and later wrote the influential book "The Bog People" (Mosefolket, 1965), argued that such deaths were ritual killings offered to a fertility goddess, pointing to the care taken with the bodies and the timing of burials in spring or early summer. Tollund Man was not alone: in 1952, workers near Grauballe, east of Silkeborg, found a second man whose throat had been slit from ear to ear, now displayed at Aarhus's Moesgaard Museum. The Elling Woman, discovered close to the Tollund Man's own bog in 1938, and the Haraldskær Woman, pulled from a Vejle bog in 1835 and once mistaken for the Viking-age queen Gunhild, add to a pattern of deliberate, violent deaths followed by deposition in water rather than cremation or grave burial, the normal rites of the period.

Preserving a body this fragile proved harder than finding it: only the head and feet of Tollund Man survive in their original state, the torso reconstructed around the preserved skeleton-less remains after conservators tried, and partly failed, to replicate the bog's own chemistry. Today he rests permanently at Museum Silkeborg, Denmark's most visited single archaeological exhibit, his calm expression still prompting the same question Glob asked: was this punishment, sacrifice, or something closer to both, in a Jutland very different from the modern Denmark around it.
