The Face as Landscape — Carl Th. Dreyer and the Cinema of the Soul

Carl Theodor Dreyer was born in Copenhagen on 3 February 1889, the illegitimate son of a Danish farmer's maid, and raised by a foster family whose surname he kept. He grew up resentful and self-taught, worked as a journalist, then joined Nordisk Films Kompagni writing title cards and scripts before directing his first feature, Præsidenten, in 1919. Over the next decades he made barely fourteen films, working in Denmark, Sweden, Germany and France, always slowly, always fighting producers for control.

His international breakthrough was the domestic drama Du skal ære din hustru (Master of the House, 1925), whose success brought French backing for La Passion de Jeanne d'Arc (1928). Built from the surviving records of Joan's 1431 trial and filmed almost entirely in close-up, with the stage actress Renée Jeanne Falconetti giving her only major screen performance, it is still routinely named among the greatest films ever made. Dreyer's original cut was long thought lost to a lab fire; a near-complete print surfaced in 1981 in a closet at a psychiatric hospital near Oslo. He followed it with the dreamlike horror film Vampyr (1932), then fell almost silent for a decade.

Under the German occupation he made Vredens Dag (Day of Wrath, 1943), a study of a 1623 witch hunt whose atmosphere of denunciation and fear was impossible to miss; Dreyer left for neutral Sweden soon after its premiere. In 1952 the Danish state granted him the licence to run the Dagmar cinema on Jernbanegade in central Copenhagen, which finally gave him a steady income. With that security he made Ordet (The Word, 1955), adapted from the pastor-playwright Kaj Munk and ending in a literal resurrection; it won the Golden Lion at Venice. His austere final film, Gertrud (1964), was jeered in Paris and later reassessed as a masterpiece.

Dreyer died in Copenhagen on 20 March 1968, his lifelong project of a film about Jesus unmade. His influence runs through Ingmar Bergman, Andrei Tarkovsky and, closer to home, the stripped-down ethics of Dogme 95. The Danish Film Institute maintains his archive and the scholarly site carlthdreyer.dk, and his features screen regularly at the Cinemateket in Copenhagen.
