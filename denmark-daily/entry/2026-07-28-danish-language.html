The Language Other Scandinavians Pretend to Understand — Danish

Danish (dansk) is a North Germanic language spoken by about six million people, and on paper it looks reassuringly close to Norwegian and Swedish — the three are cousins, and a written text is broadly shared. Spoken Danish is another matter. Even other Scandinavians joke, only half in jest, that Danish sounds like Norwegian spoken with a potato in the mouth.

The difficulty is real. Danish has an unusually rich vowel system (linguists count around 20 or more vowel sounds), and consonants are famously soft or "swallowed", so words run together and letters go unpronounced. Then there is the stød — a little catch or creak in the voice, a kind of glottal stop, that is not optional decoration but genuinely distinguishes words. Say hun ("she") without it and hund ("dog") with it, and the difference is that faint vocal break.

The numbers are the other great trap. Danish counts in a base-20 (vigesimal) system that survived where its neighbours simplified. Fifty is halvtreds — short for halvtredsindstyve, literally "half-third times twenty" (i.e. two-and-a-half twenties). Sixty is tres (three twenties), seventy halvfjerds, eighty firs, ninety halvfems. Danish children and foreigners alike simply have to memorise them.

The alphabet adds three extra letters at the end — æ, ø and å (the last officially adopted in 1948). And every learner eventually meets the national shibboleth: "rødgrød med fløde," a red berry pudding with cream whose soft ø and d sounds are nearly impossible for outsiders to reproduce, making it the classic test of a "real" Danish accent.

For all that, newcomers are rarely stuck: Danes switch to fluent English with ease. Yet learning even a little Danish — and surviving rødgrød med fløde — is one of the fastest ways to win a Dane's affection.
