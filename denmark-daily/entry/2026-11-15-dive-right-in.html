Dive Right In — How Copenhagen's Harbour Became a Swimming Pool

For most of its history, Copenhagen's harbour was a place to work, not to swim. From the Middle Ages onward, the waterway running past Slotsholmen and out toward the Sound carried merchant ships, naval vessels and, eventually, the runoff of a growing industrial city. By the twentieth century, shipyards, factories and a combined sewer system that mixed rainwater with household waste meant the harbour routinely received untreated effluent, especially after storms. Bathing in it would have been unthinkable, and for decades it was officially forbidden.

The turnaround began in the 1990s, when the City of Copenhagen committed to a systematic cleanup tied to a broader ambition: making the harbour a public amenity again rather than an industrial backwater. Engineers built large underground retention basins to hold excess storm water so it would not overflow directly into the harbour, while wastewater treatment was modernized across the city. The results were measurable. By the turn of the millennium, water quality had improved enough that officials judged it safe for the public to get in.

The symbolic turning point came in 2002, when Islands Brygge Havnebad, a free, open-air harbour bath, opened on the former dockland of Islands Brygge, just across the water from the old city center. It was designed by the young firm PLOT, the short-lived partnership of Bjarke Ingels and Julien De Smedt, who later split into the globally known practices BIG and JDS. The timber-decked pools, built directly into the harbour basin, let swimmers dive into the same water that once carried coal barges and herring boats.

Other harbour baths followed, at Fisketorvet, Sluseholmen and Nordhavn, turning the waterway into a genuine summer amenity rather than an occasional postcard view. The city continues to monitor bacterial levels daily and issues swim warnings after heavy rainfall, when the old combined-sewer overflow can still, briefly, degrade water quality. That caveat matters: the harbour's transformation is a maintained achievement, not a finished one, and it stands as a quiet marker of how Copenhagen rebuilt its relationship with the water that shaped it.
