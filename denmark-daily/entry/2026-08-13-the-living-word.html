The Living Word — N.F.S. Grundtvig and Denmark's Folk High Schools

Nikolai Frederik Severin Grundtvig (1783–1872) is arguably the single most influential mind in modern Danish culture, and one largely unknown outside it. Born in the parsonage at Udby, near Vordingborg, and trained in theology at the University of Copenhagen, he became a pastor, poet, historian and hymn writer whose output — more than 1,500 hymns, including staples still sung every Sunday in Danish churches — shaped the language itself. But his most durable legacy is an idea about how ordinary people should learn.

Grundtvig distrusted the "black school" of rote Latin, examinations and dead scholarship. He championed instead det levende ord, the living word: knowledge transmitted through spoken dialogue, storytelling, song and shared history, aimed not at credentials but at awakening a sense of national and personal belonging. Visits to England in the 1830s, where he observed Cambridge's tutorial culture, sharpened his conviction that Denmark needed schools "for life," open to farmers' sons and daughters who would never sit a university exam. He set this out most fully in his 1836 pamphlet on a Nordic university at Sorø.

The idea became institution in 1844, when the first folkehøjskole opened at Rødding in southern Jutland, close to the contested Danish-German border, founded with Grundtvig's inspiration by figures including Christian Flor. It was the schoolteacher Christen Kold, opening his own school at Ryslinge on Funen in 1851, who gave the model its lasting practical shape: boarding students through winter months, no exams, no fixed curriculum, teaching built around conversation and the teacher's personal conviction. By the century's end more than seventy folk high schools dotted the country, feeding directly into Denmark's cooperative dairy and farming movements and giving the rural population a political and cultural voice during the democratic reforms following the 1849 constitution, in whose drafting assembly Grundtvig himself sat.

Grundtvig preached at Copenhagen's Vartov Church until days before his death in 1872, and Vartov remains a center of Grundtvigian thought today. The folk high schools he inspired still operate across Denmark, offering adults short residential courses free of grades — a living argument, nearly two centuries on, that education can be about formation rather than certification.
