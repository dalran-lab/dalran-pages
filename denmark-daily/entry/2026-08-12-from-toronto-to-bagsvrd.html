From Toronto to Bagsværd — Denmark's Century of Insulin

In August 1922, the Danish physiologist August Krogh and his wife Marie sailed to North America, ostensibly so Krogh could lecture on the physiology of capillaries — work that had earned him the 1920 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. But Marie Krogh had diabetes, then a near-certain death sentence, and the couple detoured to Toronto to meet Frederick Banting and John Macleod, whose team had just isolated insulin from the pancreases of dogs. Krogh persuaded them to grant Denmark a license to manufacture the hormone, reasoning that a small country with an established pharmaceutical and biological research tradition could produce it reliably and affordably.

Back in Copenhagen, Krogh partnered with the physician Hans Christian Hagedorn and the pharmacist August Kongsted to found Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium in 1923, one of the first insulin producers outside North America. Marie Krogh became one of its earliest patients, living for decades afterward. Friction within the young company soon split it: in 1925, mechanic Harald Pedersen and his engineer brother Thorvald left to start a rival firm, Novo Terapeutisk Laboratorium, after a falling-out with Nordisk's leadership. For over sixty years the two Danish companies competed fiercely, each refining production and dosing. Hagedorn's own major contribution came in 1936 and especially 1946, when his team developed NPH insulin, a protamine-bound formulation that released more slowly into the bloodstream, sparing patients multiple daily injections.

The rivalry ended in 1989, when Novo and Nordisk merged to form Novo Nordisk, headquartered in Bagsværd, just north of Copenhagen. The merger consolidated a century of accumulated expertise in protein biochemistry and industrial fermentation, and today the company remains one of the world's largest insulin manufacturers, supplying a substantial share of the global market. That same scientific lineage — decades spent studying metabolism, hormones and the pancreas — later underpinned Novo Nordisk's development of GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide, sold as Ozempic and Wegovy, which have made the company one of Europe's most valuable and reshaped how diabetes and obesity are treated worldwide.

Krogh never profited personally from the license; he insisted the venture serve patients like his wife rather than enrich its founders. His diplomatic errand to a Toronto laboratory in 1922 remains one of the more consequential acts of scientific import in Danish history, and its two offshoot companies still define Denmark's place in global medicine.
