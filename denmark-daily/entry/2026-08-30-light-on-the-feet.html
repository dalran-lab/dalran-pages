Light on the Feet — August Bournonville and the Royal Danish Ballet

When August Bournonville took charge of the Royal Danish Ballet in 1830, he was 25 and had just walked away from a promising career in Paris. Born in Copenhagen in 1805 to Antoine Bournonville, a French-trained dancer who had settled in Denmark, he studied at the Royal Theatre's school and then in Paris under Auguste Vestris, appearing at the Paris Opéra opposite Marie Taglioni. He could have stayed. Instead he returned home and spent nearly fifty years, until his retirement in 1877, shaping a national company almost single-handedly.

The Royal Danish Ballet was not new. It traces to 1748 and the founding of the theatre on Kongens Nytorv, making it one of the oldest ballet companies in the world. What Bournonville gave it was a distinct style. Against the growing fashion for virtuoso display and the elevation of the ballerina above all others, he built choreography around buoyant, intricate footwork, soft landings, quick shifts of the head and shoulders (épaulement), and arms held low and unforced. Difficulty was hidden, not advertised. The male dancer stayed a full character rather than a lifting machine, and the ensemble mattered as much as the soloist. The mood was warm, sociable, often comic.

His surviving ballets carry the Danish Golden Age with them. La Sylphide (1836), set to music by Herman Severin Løvenskiold and made for his pupil Lucile Grahn, is the version the world now dances; the 1832 Paris original is lost. Napoli (1842), with its sunlit Bay of Naples and Act III tarantella, remains the company's signature, and A Folk Tale (1854), scored by Niels W. Gade and J.P.E. Hartmann, gave Denmark the bridal waltz still played at weddings today.

Because Bournonville taught by demonstration, the tradition passed from dancer to dancer. Around 1900 Hans Beck fixed it into six daily class sequences, the "Bournonville Schools," still drilled in Copenhagen. The company performs his work at the Old Stage on Kongens Nytorv and at the Opera House across the harbour, and mounts a periodic Bournonville Festival. Its current director, former New York City Ballet principal Nikolaj Hübbe, has both guarded and reinterpreted the repertoire. Few countries can claim a performing art so specifically their own.
