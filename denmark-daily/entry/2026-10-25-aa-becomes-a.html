Aa Becomes Å — Denmark's 1948 Spelling Reform

On 22 March 1948, Denmark's Ministry of Education issued a circular that changed the look of written Danish more than any decision in the previous century. It did two things at once. First, it formally added a twenty-ninth letter to the alphabet: å, placed after æ and ø, to replace the double vowel "aa" that had represented the same long, rounded sound in native Danish words for centuries. Overnight, "Kjøbenhavn"-era spellings like "faa" (few) and "aarhundrede" (century) became "få" and "århundrede." Second, and just as consequentially, the reform abolished the German-style convention of capitalizing every common noun, a practice Danish had inherited from centuries of close linguistic and administrative contact with German-speaking lands. From the 1948–49 school year onward, only proper names and the first word of a sentence began with a capital letter, bringing Danish into line with Norwegian and Swedish, which had dropped noun capitalization decades earlier.

The å itself was not invented in 1948; Swedish had used it since the 16th century, and some Danish writers had experimented with it earlier. What 1948 did was make it compulsory in schools and official documents, ending a messy period in which "aa" and "å" coexisted. The reform also allowed an important exception: place names and personal names already in use could keep their historic "Aa" spelling if the people or municipalities concerned chose to. Aalborg, Denmark's fourth-largest city, is the best-known holdout. Its city council reaffirmed the traditional "Aalborg" spelling as recently as 2011, treating the double-a as a piece of civic identity rather than an orthographic error. Aabenraa, in southern Jutland, made the same choice.

The 1948 reform predates the creation of Dansk Sprognævn, the Danish Language Council, which was established in 1955 and has since been the official arbiter of Danish spelling, issuing the authoritative "Retskrivningsordbog" and ruling on disputed usages. Å now sits on Danish keyboards, road signs, and the covers of books, a quiet but constant reminder that Danish spelling, like the kingdom itself, was deliberately pulled out of Germany's shadow in the twentieth century and realigned with its Nordic neighbors.
