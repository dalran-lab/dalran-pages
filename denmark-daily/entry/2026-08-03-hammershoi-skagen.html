Northern Light — Hammershøi and the Skagen Painters

Around the turn of the 20th century, Danish painting split into two opposite visions of light — one radiant and social, the other silent and grey — and both are now treasured.

At the northern tip of Jutland, where the North Sea and the Baltic collide, the fishing town of Skagen drew a colony of artists chasing its famously luminous light. The Skagen Painters worked outdoors and sociably, painting fishermen, beaches and one another. Their star was P.S. Krøyer, whose shimmering evening scenes — couples strolling the shoreline in the silvery "blue hour" — became the defining images of a Danish golden summer. Alongside him worked Michael Ancher and, greater still, his wife Anna Ancher, one of Denmark's finest painters and a master of sunlight falling through the doorways of plain rooms.

The opposite pole was a quiet man in Copenhagen. Vilhelm Hammershøi (1864–1916) painted almost the reverse of Skagen: hushed, near-empty interiors of his own apartment at Strandgade 30, rendered in a muted palette of greys, whites and black. A woman — usually his wife, Ida — often stands with her back turned; doors open onto further empty rooms; nothing happens, and it is mesmerising. Long overlooked, Hammershøi is now celebrated worldwide, his stillness feeling strikingly modern.

Behind both stood the earlier Danish Golden Age (roughly 1800–1850), when C.W. Eckersberg, the "father of Danish painting," and his gifted pupil Christen Købke brought a clear, unshowy realism to Copenhagen views and portraits.

Today the threads meet in the museums: the National Gallery of Denmark (Statens Museum for Kunst, SMK) in Copenhagen holds the great Hammershøis and Golden Age masterpieces, while the Skagens Museum keeps Krøyer's summers glowing at the top of Jutland. Between them they show how Danes have seen their own light — sometimes as a party on the beach, sometimes as silence in an empty room.
