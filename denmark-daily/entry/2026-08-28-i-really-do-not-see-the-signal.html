"I Really Do Not See the Signal — Nelson's Blind Eye off Copenhagen, 1801"

The League of Armed Neutrality, revived in December 1800 by Tsar Paul I of Russia and joined by Denmark–Norway, Sweden and Prussia, was meant to shield neutral merchant shipping from the Royal Navy's habit of stopping and searching it. London read the bloc as a pro-French move to close the Baltic, its main source of naval timber, hemp and tar. A fleet was sent north under Admiral Sir Hyde Parker, with Vice-Admiral Horatio Nelson as second-in-command.

Denmark kept no fleet at sea. Instead, a line of dismasted hulks, blockships and floating batteries was moored along the shore covering Copenhagen, anchored at its northern end by the Trekroner fort, under Commodore Olfert Fischer. The country was run by Crown Prince Frederik (the future Frederik VI), regent for his incapacitated father, Christian VII.

On the morning of 2 April 1801, Nelson led the assault division of twelve ships of the line through the shoal water; three ran aground. The fighting was close and savage. Around one o'clock, Hyde Parker, some kilometres off and unable to see clearly, hoisted Signal 39: discontinue the action. Nelson, pacing the deck of HMS Elephant, lifted his telescope to the eye blinded at Calvi in 1794 and remarked that he "really did not see the signal," telling Captain Thomas Foley: "I have only one eye. I have a right to be blind sometimes." He kept his own close-action signal flying. Within the hour the Danish line was largely silenced.

Nelson then sent a letter ashore, addressed "To the brothers of Englishmen, the Danes," warning that he would be forced to burn the captured floating batteries with their wounded crews aboard unless the firing ceased. A truce followed. Danish losses ran to roughly 1,700 killed, wounded or taken, among them the 18-year-old Peter Willemoes, whose stand aboard a floating battery made him a national hero. British casualties neared 950. Word soon came that Paul I had been assassinated in St Petersburg on 24 March; the League collapsed within weeks.

Trekroner still guards the harbour mouth, reachable by harbour boat in summer. Willemoes has streets, a warship and a Grundtvig song to his name. And the English phrase "to turn a blind eye" is still traced, fairly or not, to Nelson off Copenhagen.
