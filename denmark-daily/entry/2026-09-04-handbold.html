Håndbold — Denmark's Winter Religion and the Olympic Three-Peat

Denmark did not invent handball single-handedly, but it holds a strong claim to the paperwork. In 1906 the schoolteacher Holger Nielsen — an 1896 Olympic shooter and fencer, and later the author of a widely taught artificial-respiration method — published written rules for håndbold at Ordrup grammar school north of Copenhagen. The seven-a-side indoor version that became the world standard suited a small northern country with long winters and plentiful gymnasiums, and the game sank deep roots in provincial Denmark, especially rural Jutland, where club sport doubled as community life.

For a long time the women led. The side Danes still call de gyldne piger, "the golden girls," coached by Ulrik Wilbek and built around the mercurial playmaker Anja Andersen, won the inaugural European Championship in 1994, the World Championship in 1997, and three consecutive Olympic titles: Atlanta 1996, Sydney 2000 and Athens 2004. Two lean decades followed, broken most recently by Olympic bronze at Paris 2024 and by Team Esbjerg, which in 2024 became the first Danish women's club to win the Champions League.

The men arrived later and then would not leave. Under coach Nikolaj Jacobsen and the left back Mikkel Hansen — a three-time world player of the year whose father Flemming also wore the national shirt — Denmark won its first Olympic gold in Rio 2016, beating France 28–26. It defended the title in the delayed Tokyo final of 2021, again against France, and then in Paris 2024, overwhelming Germany 39–26. No men's team had ever won three straight Olympic handball tournaments. Alongside those golds came four consecutive world titles — 2019, 2021, 2023 and 2025 — the first won on home soil, beating Norway in the final in Herning.

Handball is comfortably Denmark's second sport after football, and during a major tournament it can feel like the first. Matches at Jyske Bank Boxen in Herning draw crowds well over 10,000; television audiences for a final can pass two million, roughly a third of the country; bars and living rooms fill on a Wednesday night in January. When Hansen retired from the national team after Paris, the succession — through young stars such as Simon Pytlick — was front-page news. For a country that rarely dominates anything globally, håndbold is the exception it has learned to expect.
