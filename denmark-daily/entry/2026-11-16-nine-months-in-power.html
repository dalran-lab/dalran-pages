Nine Months in Power — Struensee, the Mad King and the Queen's Scaffold

In 1766, fifteen-year-old Caroline Mathilde, a British princess, arrived in Copenhagen to marry Christian VII, King of Denmark and Norway. The marriage was a disaster from the start. Christian, only seventeen himself, suffered from a severe mental illness, likely a form of schizophrenia, worsened by a brutal, isolating upbringing at the hands of courtiers who had drilled discipline into him rather than affection. He oscillated between apathy, violent outbursts and nights spent in Copenhagen's brothels, leaving the queen humiliated and the court rudderless.

In 1768, a German physician named Johann Friedrich Struensee, trained in Halle and practicing in Altona, was appointed as the king's personal doctor to accompany him on a European tour. Struensee proved remarkably skilled at managing Christian's episodes, won his total trust, and by 1770 had become the de facto ruler of Denmark, governing through royal cabinet orders that bypassed the Privy Council entirely. He also became Caroline Mathilde's lover, a relationship that produced a daughter, Louise Augusta, born in 1771.

Struensee's brief ascendancy, often called the "Struensee period," unleashed a torrent of reform decrees, more than 1,000 in under two years. He abolished torture, curbed the privileges of the nobility, introduced freedom of the press, reorganized the treasury, and restructured hospitals and poor relief in Copenhagen. Much of it was genuinely progressive, decades ahead of continental norms, but it was imposed by a foreigner with no political base, alienating the nobility, the clergy and ordinary Danes who distrusted his godless rationalism and his German tongue.

The backlash came swiftly. On the night of 16–17 January 1772, a palace coup backed by the dowager queen Juliane Marie and Christian's stepbrother arrested Struensee, his ally Enevold Brandt, and Caroline Mathilde at Christiansborg Palace. Under interrogation, Struensee confessed to the affair. Convicted of lèse-majesté, he and Brandt were beheaded on Østre Fælled in Copenhagen on 28 April 1772, their right hands severed before execution. Caroline Mathilde, spared death as a foreign princess, was divorced and exiled to Celle in Hanover, where she died in 1775, aged just 23.

The episode remains one of Danish history's starkest cautionary tales about reform without legitimacy, still retold in novels, opera and film, including the 2012 film A Royal Affair.
