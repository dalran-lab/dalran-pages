A Kingdom of Islands — Denmark's Geography

Denmark is small, but it is scattered. The kingdom proper is made up of the Jutland peninsula (Jylland), which juts north from Germany, plus roughly 400 islands — around 70 of them inhabited. This archipelago geography, more than anything, explains the shape of Danish life: nowhere in the country is much more than 50 km from the sea.

The big islands carry the country's weight. Zealand (Sjælland), in the east, is the largest and holds the capital, Copenhagen, which sits on its eastern shore looking across the water to Sweden. Funen (Fyn), the "garden island," lies between Zealand and Jutland and gave the world H.C. Andersen and Carl Nielsen. Further out are Lolland and Falster to the south, and rocky Bornholm, marooned in the Baltic closer to Sweden and Poland than to the rest of Denmark.

The land itself is famously flat — the highest natural point, Møllehøj, reaches a modest 171 metres — and endlessly coastal, with some 7,300 km of shoreline between the North Sea to the west and the calmer Baltic to the east. Between the islands run the great straits: the Little Belt, the Great Belt (Storebælt) and the Øresund.

For most of history those straits divided the country; in the modern era, engineering has stitched it together. The Great Belt Fixed Link (1998) carries road and rail between Funen and Zealand across one of the world's longest suspension spans. The Øresund Bridge (2000) — a combined bridge and tunnel made famous by the Nordic-noir series The Bridge — links Copenhagen directly to Malmö in Sweden, fusing the two cities into a single cross-border "Øresund region." A new Fehmarn Belt tunnel, under construction, will soon connect the islands to Germany.

The result is a country you can drive across in a few hours yet that still feels, island to island, like a chain of distinct worlds joined by bridges and short ferry rides.
