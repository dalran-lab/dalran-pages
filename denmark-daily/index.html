Six Words on the Radio — Denmark's Occupation and the Fight to End It

At 4:15 a.m. on April 9, 1940, German troops crossed the border and landed at Copenhagen's harbor. Denmark's small army resisted for barely two hours; sixteen soldiers died before King Christian X and the government, calculating that the country was indefensible against the Wehrmacht, chose to submit under protest rather than face annihilation. What followed was unique in occupied Europe: a "peaceful occupation" in which Denmark kept its own government, police, and parliament, while Prime Minister Thorvald Stauning, and later Erik Scavenius, pursued a policy of cooperation designed to shield Danes from the brutality seen in Poland or Norway. Christian X's daily horseback rides through Copenhagen became a quiet symbol of continuity and defiance, though the famous story that he wore a yellow star himself is a myth; no Danish Jews were ever forced to wear one.

The arrangement frayed as the war turned. Illegal newspapers like Frit Danmark circulated news the censored press could not print, and by 1942 sabotage groups had begun forming, among them BOPA and the communist-linked resistance networks, later joined by the nationalist group Holger Danske. Strikes and unrest spread through 1943 until Berlin demanded the death penalty for sabotage; the Scavenius government refused, and on August 29, 1943, resigned rather than comply. The German military took direct control that same night. In the harbor, Danish naval officers scuttled much of their own fleet, thirty-two ships, to keep it from German hands. Weeks later, resistance leaders formed the Frihedsrådet, the Freedom Council, coordinating sabotage against railways and factories from the shadows for the rest of the war.

Liberation came on the evening of May 4, 1945, when a BBC broadcast announced that German forces in Denmark, the Netherlands, and northwest Germany had surrendered to Field Marshal Bernhard Montgomery. Copenhageners lit candles in their windows, a tradition still marked every May 4th across Denmark today. Roughly three thousand Danes died in the resistance or in German camps, a modest toll by continental standards, but the underground's sabotage record, and the August 1943 uprising in particular, later persuaded the Allies to recognize Denmark as a co-belligerent nation rather than a passive occupied territory.
