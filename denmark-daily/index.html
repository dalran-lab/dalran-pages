From Fossils to Faith — Nicolas Steno's Two Callings

In 1638, in a goldsmith's house in Copenhagen, a boy named Niels Stensen was born who would later be known across Europe by the Latinized name Nicolas Steno. He trained in anatomy at the University of Copenhagen under Thomas Bartholin, and by his early twenties had already identified the parotid duct in the human face — still called Stensen's duct today. But it was after he moved to Florence in 1666, entering the service of Grand Duke Ferdinando II de' Medici, that Steno made his most consequential discovery. Dissecting the head of a shark, he noticed its teeth resembled strange stony objects found embedded in Tuscan hillsides, long called "tongue stones." Steno concluded these were not minerals grown in rock but the fossilized remains of real sharks, buried when the rock itself was still soft sediment.

From that insight came his 1669 treatise, De solido intra solidum naturaliter contento, in which Steno laid out principles that would become the foundation of stratigraphy: that rock layers form in horizontal sheets, that each layer is younger than the one beneath it, and that layers extend continuously until they thin out or hit a barrier. These simple rules let geologists read the Earth's rock layers as a timeline, centuries before radiometric dating existed, and they still underpin how geology is taught today.

In 1667, while in Florence, Steno converted from Lutheranism to Catholicism, a dramatic step for a Dane of his era. He abandoned active scientific research, was ordained a priest in 1675, and was consecrated a titular bishop in 1677, serving as a vicar apostolic in northern Germany under difficult, often impoverished conditions, ministering to scattered Catholic communities. He died in 1686 in Schwerin and was buried in Florence's Basilica of San Lorenzo. In 1988, Pope John Paul II beatified him, making Steno one of the few people venerated both as a near-saint and as a founder of a modern natural science.

Denmark remembers both halves of his life: the Steno Museum in Aarhus tells the story of his science, while the Steno Diabetes Centers, Denmark's major diabetes research institutions, carry his name as a tribute to a Dane who insisted that careful observation of nature, whether rock, shark, or human body, revealed truths worth devoting a life to.
