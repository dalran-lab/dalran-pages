The Man Who Rounded the Corners — Piet Hein's Superellipse

In 1959, Swedish planners were stuck. They needed a shape for the new traffic roundabout at Sergels Torg in central Stockholm, something between a rectangle and an ellipse, smooth enough for cars to flow around but efficient enough to fit the surrounding streets. The mathematician and poet Piet Hein, born in Copenhagen in 1905, offered a solution he called the superellipse, a curve defined by raising the equation of an ellipse to a power greater than two, which flattens its sides while rounding its corners more gently than a true ellipse would. The square became kinder. The city built it, and the shape spread far beyond Scandinavian traffic planning, turning up in architecture, furniture and tableware.

Hein was never only a mathematician. He studied at the Technical University of Denmark and orbited the circle of physicists around Niels Bohr at the Institute for Theoretical Physics, absorbing their rigor while refusing to specialize. In 1933 he invented the Soma cube, a deceptively simple puzzle of seven irregular pieces that combine into a larger cube in thousands of ways, a toy that still sells today and that mathematicians still use to teach combinatorics.

During the German occupation of Denmark, Hein found a sharper use for his wit. Writing under the pseudonym Kumbel in the newspaper Politiken, he published short, epigrammatic poems called grooks, or gruk, that carried veiled defiance past Nazi censors while reading, on the surface, as playful philosophy. One of the best known runs: "Problems worthy of attack prove their worth by hitting back." Over his lifetime he wrote more than seven thousand of them, collected into volumes still in print in Denmark.

The superellipse itself went commercial when Hein partnered with the Swedish designer Bruno Mathsson and the Danish furniture maker Fritz Hansen to produce the Superellipse table, whose rounded-rectangular top let more people sit comfortably around it than a true oval could. That table remains in production, a quiet link between a Stockholm traffic problem and the Danish design tradition of solving ordinary friction with elegant, almost invisible mathematics.
