Skiing on a Power Plant — Bjarke Ingels and the Rise of BIG

Bjarke Ingels, born in Copenhagen in October 1974, trained at the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts before working under Rem Koolhaas at OMA. In 2005 he founded his own practice, BIG (Bjarke Ingels Group), built around a philosophy he calls "hedonistic sustainability": the idea that environmentally responsible architecture should also be pleasurable, even playful, rather than a form of self-denial. Two Copenhagen buildings show that philosophy at its clearest.

The first is 8 House, known in Danish as 8-Tallet, completed in 2010 in the new district of Ørestad on Amager. Shaped like a giant figure eight when seen from above, the building stacks townhouses, apartments, offices and shops into a single structure, connected by a continuous sloped path that lets residents cycle, uninterrupted, from street level to the tenth floor. The design deliberately blurs the line between a suburban row house and an urban apartment block, giving families gardens and neighbors in equal measure.

The second, more startling project is Amager Bakke, universally known as CopenHill, which opened in 2019 a short distance away on the same island. It is, at heart, a waste-to-energy plant, incinerating household rubbish from Copenhagen and turning it into electricity and district heating. But BIG wrapped the sloping roof of the power station in an artificial ski and snowboard slope, nearly 500 meters long, along with a hiking trail and Europe's tallest climbing wall on one facade. The plant's smokestack was designed to release its carbon dioxide in giant, visible rings, a literal attempt to make emissions impossible to ignore, though the mechanism has proven technically difficult to keep running reliably. Locals now ski down the roof of the very facility burning their trash, a piece of civic infrastructure turned into a mountain on a famously flat island.

Both buildings reflect a broader BIG signature: dense, functional structures reimagined as public experiences, whether that means a cycling path through an apartment block or a ski slope on a smokestack. From these Copenhagen landmarks, BIG has grown into one of the world's most prominent architecture firms, with projects from a redesigned Google headquarters in California to towers in New York and Shenzhen, but its home city remains the clearest demonstration of Ingels's conviction that sustainability and delight need not be opposites.
