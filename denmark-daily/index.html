Light Over the Sound — Eckersberg and Denmark's Golden Age of Painting

In the decades after Denmark lost its fleet to the British in 1807 and its treasury to bankruptcy in 1813, a strange thing happened in Copenhagen: its painters produced some of the clearest, calmest light in European art. Historians call the period roughly 1800 to 1850 the Danish Golden Age, and its central figure is Christoffer Wilhelm Eckersberg (1783–1853), later nicknamed the father of Danish painting. Eckersberg studied under Jacques-Louis David in Paris and absorbed a neoclassical discipline of proportion and perspective, but when he returned to Copenhagen in 1816 to teach at the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts, he turned that discipline toward unglamorous, domestic subjects: ships in the Sound, bare studio interiors, the particular slant of northern light on a plastered wall.

His students carried the method further. Christen Købke (1810–1848), who died of typhoid at thirty-seven, painted the lake at Sortedam and the ramparts of Copenhagen's old fortifications with an intimacy that made ordinary brick and water feel monumental. Wilhelm Bendz and Constantin Hansen painted fellow artists at work, candlelit and unposed, turning the studio itself into a subject. None of them were painting heroic history; they were painting the texture of a small, newly impoverished nation looking closely at what remained to it, after the loss of Norway in 1814 shrank the kingdom further still.

The timing matters. Golden Age painting coincided with Denmark's cultural retreat inward, the same decades that produced N.F.S. Grundtvig's hymns and the first stirrings of a distinctly Danish literary voice. Eckersberg's precise, almost photographic eye for ships also reflected a maritime nation rebuilding its merchant identity after the fleet's destruction.

Today the clearest place to see this is SMK, the National Gallery of Denmark, in Copenhagen's Østre Anlæg park, where an entire wing is devoted to Eckersberg, Købke and their circle. The paintings read as modest at first glance, a harbor, a window, a friend's profile, but they established a visual sensibility, understated, light-obsessed, devoted to the everyday, that modern Danish design and photography still trace their lineage to.
