The King Signs Away His Absolute Power — Denmark's Constitution of 1849

On June 5, 1849, King Frederik VII put his signature to the Junigrundloven, the June Constitution, and in doing so dismantled a system of royal absolutism that had governed Denmark since 1660. That earlier settlement, formalized in the Kongeloven (Royal Law) of 1665, had made the Danish king one of the most powerful absolute monarchs in Europe, answerable to no parliament or council. By the 1840s, revolutionary pressure sweeping across Europe, combined with a homegrown liberal and national movement, made the old order untenable. Frederik VII, reportedly more interested in hunting and his personal life than in the mechanics of governing, chose to yield rather than resist, reputedly remarking that he would keep his absolute power "as a keepsake" while handing real authority to a constitutional government.

The new constitution was drafted with major input from the jurist and later bishop Ditlev Gothard Monrad, working alongside a constituent assembly elected earlier that year. It established a bicameral parliament, the Rigsdag, split into the Folketing and the Landsting, introduced freedom of the press and religion, and guaranteed basic civil liberties, though voting rights initially excluded women, servants, and men without an independent household. The king remained head of state but now shared legislative power and could no longer rule by decree.

The document was not static. Denmark's catastrophic defeat in the 1864 war against Prussia and Austria forced a revised constitution in 1866, and further landmark changes followed: women and servants gained the vote in 1915, and the current constitution, still in force, dates to 1953, when the Landsting was abolished in favor of a single-chamber Folketing and female succession to the throne was permitted.

Danes still mark June 5 as Grundlovsdag, Constitution Day, a public half-holiday when polling stations, political tents, and open-air speeches fill parks across the country, most famously at Christiansborg Palace in Copenhagen, seat of the modern Folketing and once the residence of the absolute kings the constitution displaced. It remains one of the few days each year when Danish politics moves outdoors, back into the crowd it was written to represent.
