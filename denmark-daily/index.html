Two Nations, One Pen — Ludvig Holberg and the Comedy of Enlightenment Denmark

Ludvig Holberg was born in 1684 in Bergen, then part of the twin kingdom of Denmark-Norway, and died in Copenhagen in 1754 as a baron, a professor, and the man both nations still claim as the father of their shared literary tradition. After a restless youth of travel through the Netherlands, England, France, and Italy, he settled at the University of Copenhagen, where he taught rhetoric, then metaphysics, then history, publishing scholarly works on natural law and world history that made Danish, rather than Latin or German, a serious vehicle for ideas.

His true fame rests on comedy. In 1722 a new theatre opened on Lille Grønnegade in Copenhagen, and Holberg, inspired by Molière, supplied it with a stream of plays that turned everyday Danish speech into sharp, funny drama. Jeppe på Bjerget follows a drunken peasant tricked into believing he has become a baron; Den Politiske Kandestøber (The Political Tinker) mocks a tinsmith who fancies himself fit to run the town council; Erasmus Montanus skewers a student so puffed up with logic-chopping that he cannot admit his own mother is not a stone. These were the first comedies written in Danish for a Danish public, and they are still performed today, their satire of vanity and pretension losing little force across three centuries.

Holberg's imagination ran further still in Niels Klims underjordiske rejse (Niels Klim's Underground Travels), a 1741 satirical fantasy about a Norwegian who falls through a cave into a subterranean world of talking trees and inverted societies, a book he wrote and first published in Latin to reach a European audience while dodging Danish censors. Ennobled as Baron Holberg in 1747 by King Christian VI, he left his estate to Sorø Academy on Zealand, endowing a school that still bears his imprint.

Copenhagen has never let him go. His comedies remain core repertoire at the Royal Danish Theatre, and Bergen's Holberg Prize, an international award for outstanding scholarship in the humanities and social sciences, keeps his name attached to the very spirit of inquiry and wit he embodied.
