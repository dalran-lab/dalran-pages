The Lecture That Broke the Ice — Georg Brandes and Denmark's Modern Breakthrough

On 3 November 1871, a thirty-year-old critic named Georg Brandes stood before an overflowing hall at the University of Copenhagen and began a lecture series titled Hovedstrømninger i det 19de Aarhundredes Litteratur — Main Currents in Nineteenth-Century Literature. It is hard to overstate what followed. Brandes argued that Danish and Scandinavian literature had grown provincial, sealed off from the political and intellectual ferment sweeping France, Germany and England after 1848, and that writers had a duty to "submit problems to debate" rather than retreat into romantic escapism. The phrase became a rallying cry, and the movement it triggered acquired its own name: Det Moderne Gennembrud, the Modern Breakthrough.

Brandes was an unlikely revolutionary to Copenhagen's establishment. Born in 1842 to a secular Jewish family, sharply intelligent and formidably well read in Hegel, Taine and John Stuart Mill, he found the university's theology-adjacent literary faculty unwilling to hand him a chair; his radicalism and his religion counted against him in equal measure. For years he lectured and wrote without a formal post, until admirers raised private funds to endow him a professorship in 1902 — a telling measure of how contested, and how consequential, his influence had become.

That influence reached far past Denmark's borders. Henrik Ibsen in Norway and August Strindberg in Sweden both treated Brandes as their most serious critical interlocutor, and at home a generation of realist writers, including J.P. Jacobsen and later Nobel laureate Henrik Pontoppidan, wrote directly into the space he had opened. Brandes also had a gift for international discovery: his 1888 Copenhagen lectures on a then-obscure German philosopher, Friedrich Nietzsche, helped bring Nietzsche's work to wider European attention years before it was fashionable elsewhere.

Brandes died in 1927, by then one of the most translated critics in Europe, credited and occasionally blamed for dragging Danish letters out of romantic nostalgia and into confrontation with modern, secular, often uncomfortable ideas. The University of Copenhagen he once struggled to enter now houses an auditorium named in his honor, a quiet institutional apology for the decades it kept him waiting.
