Sold for Gold — Denmark's Caribbean Colony and the Making of the US Virgin Islands

For nearly 250 years, Denmark ruled three islands on the far side of the Atlantic: St. Thomas, settled by the Danish West India and Guinea Company in 1672; St. John, occupied in 1718; and St. Croix, bought from France in 1733. Together they formed the Danish West Indies, a sugar colony built entirely on enslaved labor. Ships sailed a triangular route from Copenhagen to Danish forts on West Africa's Gold Coast, chief among them Christiansborg (today Osu Castle, in Accra, Ghana), where captives were purchased and shipped across to plantations around Christiansted and Frederiksted on St. Croix.

Sugar, rum and cotton from these estates enriched Copenhagen merchant families and funded some of the capital's grandest townhouses. But resistance was constant, culminating on 3 July 1848, when thousands of enslaved people on St. Croix, organized under Moses "General Buddhoe" Gottlieb, marched on Frederiksted and forced Governor Peter von Scholten to declare immediate emancipation, three years before it would have taken formal effect. It remains one of the few slave emancipations in the Americas won directly by the enslaved themselves rather than granted from a distant parliament.

Denmark kept the islands as an increasingly unprofitable outpost until the First World War, when fears that a cash-strapped Denmark might lose them to a German U-boat base pushed Washington to offer $25 million in gold. A Danish referendum in December 1916 approved the sale, and on 31 March 1917 the Dannebrog was lowered over St. Thomas for the last time; the territory became the US Virgin Islands.

The story has not stayed buried. In 2017, on the transfer's centenary, Queen Margrethe II visited St. Croix and voiced regret over the "great wrongs" committed, though Denmark has stopped short of a formal apology or reparations. A year earlier, artists Jeannette Ehlers and La Vaughn Belle unveiled "I Am Queen Mary" outside the former West Indian Warehouse on Copenhagen's harbor front, a monument to Mary Thomas, one of three women who led the 1878 Fireburn labor revolt on St. Croix. It stands today as the first public monument in Denmark to the colonial history most Danes never learned in school.
