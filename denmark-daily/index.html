A Kingdom Remade — Christian III and the Reformation of 1536

On 12 August 1536, King Christian III rode into Copenhagen at the head of a victorious army and, within days, ordered the arrest of every Catholic bishop in the kingdom. It was the decisive stroke in a struggle that had smoldered since Martin Luther nailed his theses to the church door in Wittenberg in 1517. Denmark's break with Rome was not primarily a theological upheaval imposed from the pulpit; it was a political settlement forged in the ashes of civil war.

The crisis began when Christian III's predecessor and cousin, Frederik I, died in 1533, leaving the Catholic nobility and the Lutheran-leaning burghers of towns like Copenhagen and Malmø at odds over succession. The result was the Count's Feud (Grevens Fejde, 1534–1536), a brutal civil war pitting Christian III, backed by the nobility and the Duchy of Holstein, against forces loyal to the imprisoned former king Christian II and to Lübeck, which briefly tried to restore the old Catholic order. Christian III's victory, sealed by the surrender of besieged Copenhagen in the summer of 1536, gave him the leverage to act without restraint.

The bishops, who had held both spiritual authority and vast landholdings, were seized at the Herredag, the great assembly, and their sees, roughly a third of Denmark's cultivated land, were confiscated by the crown. This single act transformed the Danish monarchy's finances and power almost overnight. A new church ordinance, the Kirkeordinansen, was drafted with guidance from Luther's own colleague Johannes Bugenhagen, who traveled from Wittenberg to crown Christian III in 1537 and helped install a Lutheran superintendent structure in place of the old episcopacy. Bugenhagen also had a hand in founding the reformed University of Copenhagen's theological faculty.

The Reformation did more than change doctrine. It dissolved monasteries, redirected tithes to the crown and to poor relief, and made Danish, rather than Latin, the language of the liturgy, a shift that reinforced literacy and a shared national vernacular still traceable in the Danish spoken in Copenhagen today. Church art was stripped of many Catholic devotional images, though notable exceptions like the altarpieces in Roskilde Cathedral survive as reminders of what came before. The events of 1536 fixed Denmark's identity as a Lutheran state for the next five centuries, a legacy still visible in the state-supported Folkekirke that most Danes nominally belong to.
