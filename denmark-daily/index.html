Light as Medicine — Niels Finsen and Denmark's First Nobel Prize

In 1903, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to a physician who could barely tolerate sunlight on his own skin. Niels Ryberg Finsen was born in 1860 in Tórshavn, in the Faroe Islands, then part of the Kingdom of Denmark, the son of a Faroese governor. He studied medicine at the University of Copenhagen, graduating in 1890, and spent his short career battling a progressive illness, likely Niemann-Pick disease, that left him exhausted and swollen yet did not stop him from building an entirely new branch of medicine.

Finsen's breakthrough came from a deceptively simple observation: sunlight seemed to affect the course of certain skin diseases, and different wavelengths of light had different biological effects. He focused on lupus vulgaris, a disfiguring tuberculous infection of the skin that was common and largely untreatable at the time. Working with carbon-arc lamps that concentrated ultraviolet rays, Finsen devised a method of irradiating diseased tissue while cooling and compressing the skin with glass lenses, allowing the light to penetrate without burning. The treatment, which became known as Finsen light therapy, produced remarkable remissions in patients who had exhausted other options.

In 1896 he founded what became the Finsen Institute in Copenhagen, a dedicated phototherapy clinic that drew patients from across Europe. Seven years later, the Nobel committee recognized his work, making him the first Scandinavian to win a Nobel Prize in medicine, awarded "in recognition of his contribution to the treatment of diseases, especially lupus vulgaris, with concentrated light radiation." Finsen died in 1904, only a year after receiving the prize, at age 43, having never seen his discovery's full reach.

His institute later merged into what is now part of Copenhagen's Rigshospitalet, and the broader principle he established, that specific wavelengths of light can treat specific conditions, underlies modern treatments from neonatal jaundice phototherapy to dermatological laser medicine. A statue of Finsen stands outside the old institute buildings on Rigshospitalet's grounds, a quiet monument to a sickly Faroese-Danish doctor who turned his era's understanding of sunlight into a working clinical tool, decades before anyone understood DNA or ultraviolet damage at a molecular level.
