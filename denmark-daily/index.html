The Vote Denmark Almost Didn't Give — Women's Suffrage of 1915

On June 5, 1915, Denmark's constitution was amended to grant women the right to vote and stand for election to the Folketing, the lower house of parliament. The date mattered: June 5 was already Denmark's Constitution Day, commemorating the original 1849 document signed by Frederik VII, and tying the suffrage reform to that same calendar date framed it as a natural extension of a long democratic story rather than a rupture from it. The first elections under the new rules were held in 1918, and nine women entered the Folketing and Landsting that year, including Nina Bang, who in 1924 became the world's first female government minister with a portfolio, leading the Ministry of Education.

The campaign behind the change had been building for decades. Danish Women's Society (Dansk Kvindesamfund), founded in 1871 by Matilde Bajer and her husband Fredrik, pushed first for property and civil rights, then for the vote itself. Progress came in stages rather than one leap: women gained the right to vote in parish council elections in 1908, and in municipal elections in 1909, before the national breakthrough in 1915. Denmark was not first in the Nordic region, Finland had already granted full suffrage in 1906 and Norway in 1913, but the Danish reform was notable for extending the vote simultaneously to domestic servants and other working-class citizens, male and female alike, who had previously been excluded by property and income qualifications. This made 1915 as much a class reform as a gender one, nearly doubling the size of the electorate in a single stroke.

The timing also owed something to circumstance. Denmark remained neutral through the First World War, and the political establishment, anxious to demonstrate domestic stability while European borders burned, found it easier to extend the franchise at home than to risk social unrest. The reform passed with broad cross-party support once the Conservative opposition, long the main obstacle, finally relented.

Today the 1915 amendment is taught in Danish schools as a hinge point between the 1849 constitution's narrow, propertied democracy and the fully universal one Denmark recognizes now. Copenhagen's city hall square has hosted anniversary commemorations, and Danish politics still measures itself against that legacy: the Folketing elected in 2022 was roughly 43 percent female, among the highest shares in Europe, a statistic frequently traced back to that June morning in 1915.
