The Black Sun Over the Mudflats — Denmark's Wadden Sea

Along Denmark's southwest coast, between Esbjerg and the German border, the land does not so much end as dissolve. Twice a day the North Sea withdraws across the Wadden Sea (Danish Vadehavet, "the wading sea"), exposing more than a thousand square kilometres of sand and mud, then floods back over it. This is the largest unbroken system of intertidal sand and mud flats on Earth, running some 500 kilometres from Den Helder in the Netherlands through Germany to Ho Bugt near Esbjerg. The Dutch and German sectors became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2009; the Danish part was added on 23 June 2014, protected under a trilateral cooperation the three countries have run since the 1970s.

The Danish share forms Nationalpark Vadehavet, inaugurated on 16 October 2010 and, at about 1,466 square kilometres, the country's largest national park. It takes in the islands of Fanø, Mandø and Rømø, the tidal town of Ribe (founded around 710, Denmark's oldest), and the diked marshes of Tøndermarsken. Mandø still has no bridge: visitors cross the seabed by tractor bus, or drive the Låningsvejen only when the tide permits.

The flats are a feeding station of global importance. Ten to twelve million migratory birds use the Wadden Sea each year on the East Atlantic Flyway, fattening on worms, snails and shellfish before flights to the Arctic or West Africa. The pied avocet is the emblem of the Danish park.

The most theatrical visitors are common starlings. In March and April, and again in autumn, up to a million gather at dusk over the Tønder marshes before dropping into the reed beds to roost. Harried by marsh harriers and peregrines, the flock contracts into a single pulsing organism that darkens the sky, a spectacle Danes call sort sol, "black sun". Tours leave from Vadehavscentret near Ribe, a thatched, sculptural building rebuilt in 2017 by the Copenhagen architect Dorte Mandrup.

The coast also carries the memory of catastrophe. The Burchardi storm flood of October 1634 drowned thousands along these shores; a flood column on Ribe's quay still records how high the water climbed. Esbjerg itself was built from nothing after an 1868 harbour law, once the loss of Schleswig in 1864 cut Denmark off from Hamburg. Today that same port ships out the blades and foundations of the North Sea wind farms, making this shore both Denmark's oldest wilderness and the staging ground of its energy future.
