Reluctant Europeans — Denmark's 1972 Yes and the Birth of the Opt-Outs

On October 2, 1972, Danes voted in a referendum on joining the European Economic Community, the forerunner of today's European Union. Turnout was high and the result decisive: 63.3 percent in favor. Denmark entered on January 1, 1973, alongside Ireland and the United Kingdom, becoming the first Nordic member of what was then a club of nine. The vote followed decades of economic logic rather than continental idealism; Denmark's farmers and exporters depended heavily on British markets, and when Britain finally committed to joining, Copenhagen judged it could not afford to stay outside a bloc that included its largest trading partner.

Danish enthusiasm for the project, however, always ran shallower than in founding states like France and West Germany. That ambivalence surfaced dramatically in 1992, when Danish voters rejected the Maastricht Treaty, the agreement that transformed the EEC into the European Union and set out plans for a common currency, shared defense policy, and EU citizenship. The no vote stunned Brussels and forced a renegotiation. The result was the Edinburgh Agreement of December 1992, which carved out four Danish opt-outs: from the single currency, from common defense policy, from EU citizenship as a supranational status above national citizenship, and from cooperation on justice and home affairs. With those exemptions secured, Danes approved a revised treaty in a second referendum in May 1993.

The opt-outs have proven durable but not fixed. Danes rejected adopting the euro again in a 2000 referendum, and the krone remains pegged to it instead through the exchange rate mechanism. The justice and home affairs opt-out was narrowed in 2015 when voters declined to join Europol under the ordinary EU framework, prompting a separate parallel agreement. Most strikingly, the defense opt-out ended in June 2022, when a referendum called in the aftermath of Russia's invasion of Ukraine saw 66.9 percent of Danes vote to join the EU's Common Security and Defence Policy, ending three decades of formal distance from Brussels on military matters.

Today Denmark sits inside the EU's single market and Schengen area while retaining the krone and a case-by-case approach to Brussels' deeper political ambitions, a posture Danish politicians across the spectrum describe as pragmatic rather than reluctant. Queen Margrethe II presided over the country through the entire arc from 1972 accession to the 2022 defense vote, a reign that traced Denmark's slow, referendum-driven negotiation of its place in Europe.
