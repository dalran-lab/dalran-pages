The Eclipse That Was Late — Ole Rømer and the Speed of Light

In 1676, the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer stood before the Royal Academy of Sciences in Paris and announced something that sounded almost absurd: light takes time to travel. Until then, most natural philosophers, including Descartes, assumed light's speed was effectively infinite, arriving everywhere at once. Rømer's evidence came from an unglamorous source, years of timing the eclipses of Io, one of Jupiter's four large moons discovered by Galileo. Io orbits Jupiter with such clockwork regularity that astronomers used its eclipses, the moments it slipped into Jupiter's shadow, as a kind of cosmic clock, useful for calculating longitude at sea.

Working at the Paris Observatory under Giovanni Domenico Cassini, Rømer noticed something odd in years of accumulated data: the eclipses ran late when Earth was moving away from Jupiter in its orbit, and early when Earth was approaching. The discrepancy built up to about 22 minutes over roughly six months, the time it took Earth to cross from one side of the Sun to the other. Rømer's explanation was elegant. Light was not instantaneous. It had a finite speed, and the extra distance light had to cross when Earth was farther from Jupiter accounted for the delay. The Dutch physicist Christiaan Huygens soon used Rømer's timing data, combined with an estimate of Earth's orbital diameter, to produce the first numerical estimate of light's speed, too slow by modern standards but the right order of magnitude, and the right idea.

Rømer, born in Aarhus in 1644 and educated at the University of Copenhagen, returned home in 1681 to a distinguished career: professor of astronomy, director of the Round Tower observatory in Copenhagen, and eventually mayor of the city and head of the Danish royal mint, where he introduced Denmark's first standardized system of weights and measures. He is also credited, independently of Fahrenheit, with an early temperature scale.

Today the Ole Rømer Museum near Aarhus preserves the site of his family's farm and his astronomical instruments, a quiet monument to a Danish eclipse-watcher who first proved that even light needs time to arrive.
