Chiselled Marble, Danish Fame — Bertel Thorvaldsen's Homecoming

When Bertel Thorvaldsen sailed into Copenhagen harbor on 17 September 1838, cannons fired salutes and crowds lined the quays as though a king had returned. In a sense one had: Europe's most celebrated living sculptor, rival to the Italian Antonio Canova, was coming home after four decades in Rome. Born in Copenhagen in 1770 to a poor Icelandic wood-carver father and a Danish mother, Thorvaldsen trained at the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts and won its prestigious travel scholarship in 1793. He reached Rome in 1797 and, apart from occasional trips, stayed there until his return, absorbing classical antiquity so thoroughly that his 1803 statue Jason with the Golden Fleece convinced patrons he had surpassed his teachers. Commissions poured in from across Europe: the marble frieze and statues for Vor Frue Kirke, Copenhagen's cathedral, including his solemn Christus and the twelve apostles; the tomb of Pope Pius VII inside St. Peter's Basilica; a monument to Nicolaus Copernicus in Warsaw; and the mournful Lion Monument carved into a Lucerne cliff face to honor Swiss Guards killed defending Louis XVI in 1792.

Rather than let his life's work scatter across foreign collections, Thorvaldsen gave nearly everything, his sculptures, models, and a personal collection of Egyptian, Greek, and Roman antiquities and paintings, to his native city. The architect Gottlieb Bindesbøll designed a purpose-built museum beside Christiansborg Palace to house it, one of the earliest buildings in Europe conceived from the ground up as a public art museum. Construction began in 1839, while Thorvaldsen was still alive to advise on the layout, and the building opened in 1848, four years after his death. Its exterior remains startling even today: ochre, red, and grey friezes running the length of the facade depict the artist's triumphant ship arriving in Copenhagen, a scene of civic pride rendered directly onto stucco.

Thorvaldsen died suddenly in March 1844 while attending the Royal Theatre, and he is buried not in a churchyard but in the museum's open inner courtyard, beneath a plain slab and ivy, surrounded permanently by his own work. The Thorvaldsens Museum still stands there today, a short walk from Christiansborg, holding the largest collection of his sculpture anywhere and offering Copenhagen a direct, tactile link to the neoclassical taste that once made a shoemaker's grandson the toast of Rome.
