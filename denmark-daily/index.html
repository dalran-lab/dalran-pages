Scuttled to Block a Fjord — Roskilde's Skuldelev Viking Ships

Around the year 1070 the people of Roskilde faced the sea with dread. Their town was then the royal seat of Denmark, its cathedral already a burial church for kings, and it lay at the head of a long, shallow fjord. To close the fastest channel, the Peberrende, against raiders, they filled five worn-out ships with stones and sank them across it. The barrier held, was later reinforced, and then forgotten for nine centuries.

In 1957 divers confirmed the wrecks were Viking-age. In 1962 the archaeologists Ole Crumlin-Pedersen and Olaf Olsen ringed the site with a steel cofferdam, pumped it dry, and lifted tens of thousands of waterlogged oak and pine fragments by hand over four months. Painstakingly reassembled, the pieces became the Skuldelev ships, named for the nearest village.

They proved not to be a single fleet but a cross-section of Norse seafaring. Skuldelev 1 is a sturdy ocean trader, a knarr built in western Norway around 1030, the type that supplied Greenland. Skuldelev 3 is a trim Danish coastal cargo boat. Skuldelev 5 is a modest local warship. Skuldelev 2 is the revelation: a roughly 30-metre oak longship built near Dublin about 1042, crewed by sixty or more, hard proof of the Irish Sea's place in the Danish world. Skuldelev 6 began life as a Norwegian fishing and utility vessel.

To house them, Denmark built the Vikingeskibshallen, a stark waterfront hall of raw concrete by the architect Erik Christian Sørensen, opened in 1969 on Roskilde harbour. It remains one of the country's boldest modernist buildings, though rising storm surges, notably the Bodil storm of December 2013 that flooded the galleries, have pushed the museum toward a planned replacement.

The institution is not a static display. On its Museum Island, boatbuilders use Viking-era tools to construct full-scale reconstructions and then sail them. The Sea Stallion from Glendalough, a copy of Skuldelev 2, sailed from Roskilde to Dublin in 2007 with a crew of sixty-five, testing the original's speed and its discomfort at sea.

Digging the museum's own harbour basin in 1996 and 1997 uncovered nine further wrecks, among them Roskilde 6, at about 37 metres the longest Viking ship yet found, built after 1025 and since exhibited at the British Museum and the National Museum of Denmark.
