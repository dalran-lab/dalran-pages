The Castle That Guarded a Toll — Kronborg and the Sound Dues

At the narrowest point of the Øresund, where only four kilometres of water separate Denmark from Sweden, stands Kronborg Castle, one of the most strategically calculated buildings in northern Europe. Its predecessor, a fortress called Krogen, was raised in the 1420s by King Eric of Pomerania for a single, lucrative purpose: forcing every merchant ship passing between the Baltic and the North Sea to lower its sails, anchor, and pay a toll, the Sound Dues, or face cannon fire from the ramparts. That toll, collected without interruption from 1429 until 1857, became one of the Danish crown's most reliable sources of income, funding wars, palaces, and the ambitions of kings who otherwise ruled a modest realm.

Frederik II transformed the old fortress between 1574 and 1585 into the Renaissance showpiece that stands today, employing the Dutch architects Hans van Paaschen and Anthonis van Opbergen to give Kronborg the spires, sandstone facades, and copper roofs meant to impress every foreign captain forced to pay tribute beneath its walls. A fire in 1629 gutted much of the interior during the reign of Christian IV, who rebuilt it, and Swedish forces occupied the castle during the 1658 war, carrying off tapestries and silver still held in Swedish collections today.

Kronborg's fame outside Denmark rests less on the toll than on a play its builder never saw performed there. William Shakespeare, who likely never set foot in Denmark, set Hamlet at "Elsinore," almost certainly having heard of the castle from English actors who toured and performed at Kronborg in the 1580s. The identification stuck so firmly that the castle now hosts an annual Hamlet festival each August, staged in its courtyards.

Deep in Kronborg's casemates sits a stone statue of Holger Danske, a legendary warrior said to sleep until Denmark faces mortal danger, when he will awaken to defend the kingdom, a folk image invoked by the Danish resistance during the German occupation. Since 2000, Kronborg has held UNESCO World Heritage status, and just below its walls the Danish Maritime Museum, designed by Bjarke Ingels' firm BIG and opened in 2013, was built entirely underground so as not to compete with the old toll-keeper's silhouette against the sound.
