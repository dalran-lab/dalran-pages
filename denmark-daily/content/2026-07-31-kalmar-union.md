For more than a century, a single crown ruled almost all of Scandinavia — and the ruler who built it was a woman written out of most of the official king-lists.

**Margrete I** (1353–1412) was the daughter of King Valdemar IV of Denmark. Married young to the king of Norway, widowed, and left to defend her son's claims, she proved one of the shrewdest political operators of the Middle Ages. Through inheritance, diplomacy and hard power she brought **Denmark, Norway and Sweden** together, and in **1397**, in the Swedish town of **Kalmar**, the three realms were formally joined in the **Kalmar Union**. Her great-nephew **Erik of Pomerania** was crowned as nominal king, but everyone knew who ruled: Margrete governed as regent until her death, an uncrowned queen more powerful than most kings.

The union was always a loose personal one — three kingdoms sharing a monarch while keeping their own laws — and it strained constantly, above all over Danish dominance and Swedish resentment. It limped on for over a century until **1523**, when **Gustav Vasa** led Sweden permanently out of the union. The final rupture followed one of Scandinavia's most notorious atrocities: the **Stockholm Bloodbath of 1520**, in which the Danish king **Christian II** executed scores of Swedish nobles, extinguishing any goodwill that remained.

Denmark and Norway, however, stayed bound together as a single state until **1814**, when Norway was handed to Sweden in the aftermath of the Napoleonic Wars. Margrete herself was laid to rest in **Roskilde Cathedral**, the royal burial church near Copenhagen where Danish monarchs still lie today.

Her legacy is twofold: she remains a symbol of a road not taken — a united Scandinavia — and a reminder that some of medieval Europe's most effective rulers governed from behind a nephew's throne.
