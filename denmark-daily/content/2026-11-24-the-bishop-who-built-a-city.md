In 1167, a warrior-bishop named **Absalon** raised a fortress on a small island in the Øresund strait, at a natural harbor long used by fishermen. That stronghold, built to guard against raids by Wendish pirates from the southern Baltic coast, stands at the origin point of what would become **Copenhagen**, Denmark's capital. The settlement already had a name, Havn, meaning simply "harbor," and later København, "merchants' harbor," reflecting the trade that grew around it.

Absalon was no ordinary cleric. Born around 1128 into the powerful Hvide family, he was raised alongside the future king **Valdemar I**, forming a friendship that shaped Danish politics for decades. He became Bishop of Roskilde in 1158 and later Archbishop of Lund, but he is remembered above all as a statesman and military commander who fused ecclesiastical authority with royal power during a turbulent century when Denmark faced both external threats from Wendish raiders and internal struggles between rival claimants to the throne.

The fortress Absalon commissioned, built of granite boulders on the islet now called Slotsholmen, gave the merchants' harbor the protection it needed to flourish. Danish King Valdemar had granted Absalon the harbor and surrounding land, and the bishop's military investment transformed a modest fishing hamlet into a defensible trading post. Ships could dock safely, goods could move, and a town grew up around the castle walls. That same site today holds **Christiansborg Palace**, home to the Danish Parliament, the Supreme Court, and the Prime Minister's offices, meaning Denmark's seat of government still sits precisely where Absalon's fortress once stood.

Our understanding of Absalon's life comes substantially from the chronicler **Saxo Grammaticus**, who served in his household and later wrote *Gesta Danorum*, the great Latin history of the Danes, partly at Absalon's urging. Absalon died in 1201 at the monastery of Sorø, where his tomb can still be visited.

A bronze equestrian statue of Absalon, raised in 1902, stands today at Højbro Plads in central Copenhagen, sword in one hand, a model of his church in the other, overlooking the canal he helped bring into being, a quiet reminder that the city's prosperity began with a fortress built against pirates.
