When German troops crossed the Danish border before dawn on 9 April 1940, **King Christian X** was 70 years old and had reigned since 1912. The government capitulated within hours, judging armed resistance hopeless against a neighbour many times Denmark's size. What the King could still offer was presence. Almost every morning through the occupation he rode out from **Amalienborg Palace** on horseback, alone or nearly so, through the inner city and along the lakes, returning the greetings of ordinary Danes who lifted their hats or stopped on the pavement to watch him pass.

The rides carried no military weight, but as daily theatre they were potent. A much-repeated story has a German soldier asking a boy why the elderly rider had no bodyguard, and the boy answering that all of Denmark was his guard. The tale is probably apocryphal; the sentiment was not. On the King's 70th birthday, 26 September 1940, hundreds of thousands of Danes pinned on the **Kongemærket**, a small badge bearing his crowned monogram, as a wordless assertion of national unity.

Christian X was an unlikely figure for this role. Conservative and stiff, he had nearly wrecked the monarchy in the **Easter Crisis of 1920**, dismissing an elected government and pushing Denmark to the brink of becoming a republic before he backed down; thereafter the Crown was purely ceremonial. He was also the king who in 1920 had ridden a white horse across the old frontier into reunified **North Schleswig**, an image the wartime rides deliberately echoed.

His curt reply to a long, fawning birthday telegram from Hitler in September 1942, essentially "my best thanks" and nothing more, set off the **Telegramkrisen**. Relations collapsed, and the hardliner **Werner Best** was sent to Copenhagen as Reich plenipotentiary. On 19 October 1942 the King fell from his horse during his morning ride and was badly injured; he never fully recovered, and his public rounds effectively ended.

The popular legend that Christian X wore, or threatened to wear, a yellow star in solidarity with Danish Jews is false. Danish Jews were never forced to wear one, and most were ferried to Sweden in October 1943. But the myth grew from something real: a monarch who turned a horse and a fixed morning route into a form of quiet defiance. His great-grandson **Frederik X** lives at Amalienborg today; an equestrian statue of Christian X stands in central Aarhus.
