In the decades after Denmark's state bankruptcy in 1813 and the loss of Norway in 1814, a country stripped of empire and money produced an unlikely flowering of painting. Art historians later called it the Golden Age, roughly 1800 to 1850, and its central figure was **Christoffer Wilhelm Eckersberg**, who taught at the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts in Copenhagen from 1818 until his death in 1853. Eckersberg had studied in Paris under Jacques-Louis David and in Rome, absorbing Neoclassical discipline, but he turned that training toward something quieter: ships in the Sound, nudes rendered with anatomical precision, and the particular grey-blue light of Danish mornings. He insisted his students work from direct observation rather than idealized convention, a method that reshaped Danish painting for a generation.

Among his pupils, **Christen Købke** became the Golden Age's most beloved painter, though recognition came mostly after his early death in 1848 at age 37. Købke painted Copenhagen's fortifications, the lake at Sortedam, and friends and family with an intimacy that feels startlingly modern, small scenes elevated by exact attention to light falling on brick and water. Other Eckersberg students, including **Wilhelm Bendz** and **Constantin Hansen**, extended this vocabulary into portraiture and scenes of student life, often set in Rome, where a Danish art colony clustered around the sculptor Bertel Thorvaldsen's reputation.

The Golden Age coincided deliberately with national hardship. Stripped of its fleet after the British bombardment of 1807 and of Norway by the Treaty of Kiel, Denmark turned inward, and its artists found grandeur in modest subjects: a kitchen maid, a view from a window, a single frigate at anchor. This was not provincial retreat but a considered aesthetic, one that prefigured the pared-back sensibility later associated with Danish design.

Today the **Statens Museum for Kunst** (SMK) in Copenhagen holds the largest collection of these works, including numerous Eckersbergs and Købkes, free to view in its permanent collection. The Golden Age also shaped the Royal Danish Academy's teaching traditions, which persist in the same Charlottenborg building on Kongens Nytorv where Eckersberg once taught. Walking that stretch of the city today, past the harbor light he spent a lifetime studying, it is easy to see why he never left.
