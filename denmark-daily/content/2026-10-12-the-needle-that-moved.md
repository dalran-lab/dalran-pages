On a spring afternoon in 1820, during a lecture at the University of Copenhagen, **Hans Christian Ørsted** noticed something that would reshape physics: a compass needle near a wire carrying electric current swung out of alignment with magnetic north. It was not the first time he had looked for a connection between electricity and magnetism. Ørsted had suspected one existed for years, inspired by the Naturphilosophie tradition that treated nature's forces as fundamentally unified, but this was the first time anyone had demonstrated it experimentally. He published his findings in July 1820 in a four-page Latin paper, "Experimenta circa effectum conflictus electrici in acum magneticam," and sent it directly to scientific societies across Europe rather than waiting for a journal. Within weeks, French physicists including **André-Marie Ampère** had built on the discovery, formalizing the relationship between current and magnetic force that became the field of electromagnetism. Ørsted's observation is often cited as the single most consequential moment in the unification of electricity and magnetism, a link that **James Clerk Maxwell** would later express in the equations underlying everything from electric motors to radio waves.

Ørsted was born in 1777 in Rudkøbing on the island of Langeland, and trained as a pharmacist before turning to physics and chemistry at the University of Copenhagen, where he became a professor in 1806. Beyond the 1820 discovery, he spent years attempting to isolate the metal aluminum, producing an impure form of aluminum chloride in 1825 that pointed the way toward the element's later isolation by others. He was also a tireless institution builder: in 1829 he founded what is now the Technical University of Denmark (DTU) in Copenhagen, arguing that Denmark needed an institution training engineers in applied science, not just theorists sitting in lecture halls.

His name endures in daily use well beyond physics classrooms. The oersted, a unit of magnetic field strength in the older CGS system, bears his name, as does Ørsteds Plads near DTU's Copenhagen campus. Most visibly to modern Danes, the state-controlled energy company once called DONG Energy renamed itself **Ørsted** in 2017 as it pivoted away from oil and gas toward offshore wind power, deliberately invoking a scientist whose chance observation, nearly two centuries earlier, had helped set the stage for the generators now turning in the North Sea.
