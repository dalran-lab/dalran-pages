In 1676, a 32-year-old Danish astronomer working at the Paris Observatory announced something that quietly rewired physics: light does not travel instantaneously. **Ole Rømer**, born in Århus in 1644 and educated at the University of Copenhagen under the physicist **Rasmus Bartholin**, had spent years timing the eclipses of Jupiter's innermost moon, Io, as it slipped behind the giant planet. Working alongside **Jean Picard** and using the long observational record of **Giovanni Domenico Cassini**, Rømer noticed something odd: the eclipses ran late when Earth was moving away from Jupiter in its orbit, and early when Earth approached it. The gap could reach about 22 minutes across a year. Rømer argued the only explanation was that light itself needed time to cross the extra distance. He presented the idea to the French Academy of Sciences, and it was **Christiaan Huygens** who turned Rømer's timing data into the first rough numerical estimate of light's speed, a figure that was off from the modern value but correct in principle, and correct a full generation before Newton's *Opticks* treated light as instantaneous in some editions.

Rømer did not stay abroad. He returned to Denmark in 1681 and took charge of the observatory atop **Rundetårn**, the round tower in central Copenhagen that still stands beside Trinitatis Church, a short walk from the university he had once studied at. There his ambitions broadened well past astronomy. As the city's police chief and later mayor, he standardized Danish weights and measures, oversaw the city's first fire service reforms, and designed Copenhagen's earliest oil street lamps, a practical civic legacy as tangible as his astronomical one. He also devised a thermometer scale, the Rømer scale, built around the freezing and boiling points of water and brine, which the German instrument maker Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit studied directly before creating his own, more famous scale in the 1710s.

Much of Rømer's original instrumentation was lost in the great Copenhagen Fire of 1728, which gutted Rundetårn's observatory along with large parts of the old city. Yet the tower still functions as one of Europe's oldest working observatories open to the public, and Rømer's basic insight, that light has a finite, measurable speed, remains a foundation of modern physics, confirmed to extraordinary precision but never overturned.
