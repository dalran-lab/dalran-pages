In 1925, two young engineers, **Peter Bang** and **Svend Olufsen**, began building radio equipment in the attic of the Olufsen family farmhouse near **Struer**, a small town on the Jutland coast. Bang had studied at the Technical University of Denmark and briefly worked in the United States; Olufsen brought capital and a farmhouse workshop. Their early radios ran on alternating current at a time when most competitors still relied on batteries, and this technical edge let the fledgling company, **Bang & Olufsen**, carve out a market even through the Depression and the German occupation of Denmark during the Second World War, when the factory was sabotaged rather than surrendered to war production.

What distinguishes Bang & Olufsen from ordinary electronics manufacturers is a decision made in the 1960s to treat the object itself, not just its circuitry, as the product. In 1965 the company brought in the industrial designer **Jacob Jensen**, who over the following decades authored more than two hundred products defined by anodized aluminum, brushed steel, glass, and a near-total suppression of visible buttons and clutter. His 1972 turntable, the **Beogram 4000**, used a touch-sensitive tangential tonearm controlled by lightly grazing a metal panel, and it entered the permanent design collection of the Museum of Modern Art in New York, an unusual honor for a piece of consumer electronics. Jensen's guiding principle, that a product should reveal its function through form rather than through instructions, drew directly on the same Danish Modern lineage that shaped Hans Wegner's chairs and Poul Henningsen's lamps, but applied it to motors, speakers, and circuit boards.

This philosophy made Bang & Olufsen as much a fixture of Danish design museums as of Danish living rooms. Its products appear in the permanent collections of Design Museum Denmark in Copenhagen alongside furniture and silverware, treated not as gadgets but as sculpture that happens to play music. The company weathered near-collapse in the 1990s and again after the 2008 financial crisis, as digital competitors undercut its premium hardware, and it has since repositioned around luxury audio, licensing its acoustic and design expertise to automotive partners while keeping final assembly in Struer.

Walk into a Bang & Olufsen showroom on Copenhagen's Strøget today, and the continuity is obvious: speakers still stand like minimalist totems, their form uncluttered by visible screws or seams, a century-old argument that engineering and beauty were never separate problems to begin with.
