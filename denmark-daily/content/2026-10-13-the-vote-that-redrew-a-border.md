When Germany lost the First World War, the peace settlement gave Denmark something it had failed to win by force in 1864: a chance to reclaim Schleswig by ballot instead of bayonet. Article 109 of the Treaty of Versailles, signed in June 1919, ordered a plebiscite in the Danish-German borderland, split into two voting zones so that each district's wishes, rather than a single regional average, would decide its fate.

On **10 February 1920**, Zone 1, stretching from Tønder and Aabenraa to Sønderborg and Haderslev, voted roughly three to one in favor of joining Denmark. A month later, on **14 March 1920**, Zone 2, centered on Flensburg, voted by a similarly lopsided margin to remain part of Germany. The border that emerged split the old duchy of Schleswig almost exactly where language and sentiment, not any natural frontier, divided the population, leaving Flensburg's Danish minority on the German side, a wound still felt in local politics today.

The man most associated with the Danish cause was **H.P. Hanssen**, a farmer's son from Sønderborg who had spent two decades as the elected voice of North Schleswig's Danes inside the German Reichstag, arguing their case from within the very parliament that had annexed them. His patient, legalistic advocacy helped keep Danish identity alive through 56 years of Prussian rule and positioned the region to seize the postwar opening.

Reunification was sealed on **10–11 July 1920**, when King **Christian X** rode across the old 1864 border near Christiansfeld on his white horse, a deliberately staged image of a king reclaiming lost ground without a shot fired. Crowds gathered at **Dybbøl Banke**, the same hill where Danish troops had been overrun by Prussian artillery two generations earlier, to watch the Dannebrog raised over soil that had been German since Bismarck's war.

The episode remains a rare case of a European border settled by democratic vote rather than conquest, and it still shapes Danish-German relations: a protected Danish minority persists around Flensburg, a German minority remains in Aabenraa and Sønderborg, and both are treated today as a model of peaceful coexistence rather than lingering grievance.
