**Christian IV**, who ruled Denmark and Norway from 1588 to 1648, left a deeper physical mark on Copenhagen than any monarch before or since. Crowned at eleven years old and reigning for fifty-nine years, he came to power with a treasury swollen by Sound Dues revenue and a conviction that his capital should look the part of a great European power. The result was a building campaign of extraordinary scale, much of which still defines the city's historic core.

**Rosenborg Castle**, begun in 1606 as a modest summer pleasure house in what was then open parkland, grew under Christian IV's restless attention into the red-brick Dutch Renaissance palace that now sits inside Kongens Have, housing the Danish crown jewels. Nearby, he founded **Rundetårn**, Europe's oldest functioning astronomical observatory, completed in 1642 with its distinctive internal spiral ramp rather than stairs, reportedly so a horse-drawn cart, or legend has it a coach, could reach the top. On the harbor, his stock exchange **Børsen**, finished in 1640, became famous for a spire of four intertwined dragon tails, a flourish typical of his taste for the theatrical. He also laid out **Christianshavn**, a new planted town across the harbor modeled on Dutch canal cities, and built **Nyboder**, rows of modest yellow houses for the navy's sailors and their families, some of which are still inhabited today.

Christian IV's ambitions were not only architectural. He pursued an aggressive foreign policy that often outpaced Denmark's actual strength. His intervention in the Thirty Years' War ended in disaster at the **Battle of Lutter am Barenberge** in 1626, where his forces were crushed by Catholic League troops under Tilly, forcing a humiliating retreat and exposing the limits of Danish power on the European stage. Earlier, the Kalmar War of 1611 to 1613 against Sweden had gone better, but the long-term rivalry between the two Nordic kingdoms only deepened.

Today, walking from Rosenborg to Børsen to Rundetårn traces the ambitions of a single reign. Copenhagen's tourist board still markets this route as the "Christian IV walk," and his initials, a crowned C4, appear on buildings across the old town, a quieter but durable legacy than the wars he lost.
