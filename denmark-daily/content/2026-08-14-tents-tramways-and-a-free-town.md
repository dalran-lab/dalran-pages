On September 26, 1971, a group of squatters broke through a fence around an abandoned military barracks in Christianshavn, the area known as Bådsmandsstræde, and declared it open territory. Within days, the journalist **Jacob Ludvigsen** published a manifesto in his underground paper Hovedbladet titled "Emigrer med sporvognen" ("Emigrate with the tramway"), inviting Copenhageners to settle the vacant ramparts rather than travel abroad to find utopia. He gave the settlement its name: **Christiania**, a play on the old royal habit of naming things after kings named Christian, now claimed as a "free town."

The site itself had history behind it. **Christianshavns Vold**, the star-shaped fortification ring built in the 17th century under Christian IV, had housed Danish army barracks until the military abandoned them in 1971, leaving 34 hectares of empty buildings, ammunition depots and green ramparts inside the city. The vacuum was irresistible to a generation shaped by 1968 and the housing shortages of postwar Copenhagen.

Christiania developed its own institutions: a common law forbidding cars, guns and hard drugs, consensus-based town meetings, self-built housing, and a flag of red and yellow bearing three dots, borrowed, according to local lore, from the dots over the i's in a nearby café's sign. The area's most contested feature became **Pusher Street**, an open-air cannabis market that operated for decades in defiance of Danish law, drawing both tourists and periodic police raids, including major crackdowns in 2004 and after a fatal shooting during a 2016 police action.

The community's legal status shifted decisively in 2011, when residents and the Danish state agreed on the so-called Christiania deal: a foundation, **Fonden Freetown Christiania**, would purchase the land and buildings collectively rather than face individual privatization, preserving communal ownership under Danish law. Today roughly 900 people live there, and Christiania remains one of Copenhagen's most visited sites, a living argument about property, autonomy and what a Danish welfare state tolerates at its own center, even as residents themselves dismantled Pusher Street's stalls in 2024 to distance the community from escalating gang violence linked to the drug trade.
