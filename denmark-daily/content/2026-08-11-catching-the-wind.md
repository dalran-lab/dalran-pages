In 1891, at the Askov Folk High School in southern Jutland, physicist **Poul la Cour** began experimenting with wind as a source of electricity. La Cour, who had trained under Danish scientist H.C. Ørsted's intellectual tradition of rigorous experimentation, built a wind tunnel to test rotor blade shapes scientifically rather than by guesswork, and used surplus wind-generated power to run electrolysis, splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen for storage and gas lighting. In 1905 he founded the Society of Danish Wind Electricians (Dansk Vindelektricitetsselskab) and published the journal *Vind og Vejr*, training a generation of rural electricians. By the 1900s hundreds of Danish farms had small wind-electric plants, a uniquely Danish head start that no other country matched at the time.

That lineage continued through **Johannes Juul**, a former la Cour student, who in 1956–57 built the Gedser wind turbine on the southern tip of Falster. Its three-bladed, stall-regulated design with an asynchronous generator, connected directly to the electricity grid, became known as the "Danish concept" and quietly ran until 1967. When the 1973 oil crisis exposed Denmark's near-total dependence on imported fuel, engineers and grassroots cooperatives revisited Juul's blueprint. Teachers and students at the Tvind schools built a large, community-funded turbine between 1975 and 1978, partly as a protest against nuclear power, while Denmark's small agricultural machine shops began retooling.

One of those shops, a smithy founded in 1898 in the Jutland town of Lem, became **Vestas**, which pivoted from farm equipment to wind turbines in 1979 and grew into the world's largest turbine manufacturer, still headquartered in Aarhus.

The offshore chapter opened in 1991 at Vindeby, off the coast of Lolland, where the state-owned oil and gas company DONG Energy helped build the world's first offshore wind farm. As turbines multiplied — visible today from Copenhagen's harbor at the community-owned Middelgrunden array — the company progressively divested its fossil fuel business and, in 2017, renamed itself **Ørsted**, after the same H.C. Ørsted whose electromagnetic discoveries had once inspired la Cour. Today wind routinely supplies over half of Denmark's electricity, a direct descendant of one professor's attic experiments in Askov.
