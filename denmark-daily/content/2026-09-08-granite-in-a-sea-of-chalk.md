Bornholm sits about 150 kilometres east of the rest of Denmark, closer to the Swedish coast of Skåne and to Poland than to Zealand. It is a geological anomaly. While the rest of Denmark is built of soft glacial clay, sand and chalk, Bornholm is a slab of granite and gneiss, the northern edge of the Fennoscandian Shield breaking the surface of the Baltic. Quarrymen have cut that rock for centuries, and Bornholm granite paves streets and faces buildings across **Copenhagen**. The south of the island softens into sandstone, shale and the fine white dunes of **Dueodde**.

The island's signature buildings are its four round churches, the rundkirker, raised in the 12th century at **Østerlars**, **Nylars**, **Nyker** and **Olsker**. **Østerlars Kirke**, the largest, dates from around 1150. Their walls, metres thick, and their upper defensive galleries mark them as fortresses as much as sanctuaries, refuges for farmers and their livestock when Wendish pirates raided from the Slavic Baltic coast. For much of the Middle Ages Bornholm belonged not to the Danish crown but to the Archbishop of Lund, whose stronghold, **Hammershus**, still commands the northern cliffs as the largest medieval fortification ruin in northern Europe.

Bornholm's Danishness was a choice. The 1658 Treaty of Roskilde handed the island to Sweden, but within months a rising led by **Jens Kofoed** killed the Swedish commander **Johan Printzensköld** and returned Bornholm to King **Frederik III**. It has stayed Danish ever since, with one strange coda: after the German garrison refused to surrender to the Red Army, Soviet aircraft bombed **Rønne** and **Nexø** on 7 and 8 May 1945, and Soviet troops then occupied the island until April 1946.

Then there is the herring. The tall white chimneys of the island's smokehouses, the røgerier, still cure Baltic herring over alder smoke, especially around **Gudhjem**. The classic plate, "Sol over Gudhjem", sun over Gudhjem, is a warm smoked herring on dark rye with a raw egg yolk, radishes and chives. That larder, plus the clearest light and the most sunshine hours in Denmark, drew painters such as **Oluf Høst** and, more recently, made Bornholm one of the country's most ambitious food regions, branding itself the "Bright Green Island" with a pledge to become waste-free and carbon-neutral.
