Before he was a saint, **Niels Steensen** was a Copenhagen-born anatomist who, almost by accident, founded modern geology. Born in 1638, the son of a royal goldsmith, he trained in medicine at the University of Copenhagen before traveling to Leiden and then to Italy, where he found his most important patron: **Ferdinando II de' Medici**, Grand Duke of Tuscany. In Florence, Steno's early fame rested on anatomy, he identified the parotid salivary duct, still called **Stensen's duct**, and studied the structure of muscle and the heart with unusual rigor for his time.

It was a shark's head, dissected in 1666 near Livorno, that redirected his career. Its teeth closely resembled so-called "tongue stones" found embedded in Tuscan rock, objects long debated as either minerals or petrified remains of living creatures. Steno concluded they were fossilized teeth, and from that single observation he built a far broader argument about how solid rock itself forms and records history. In his 1669 treatise, with the famously unwieldy title *De solido intra solidum naturaliter contento dissertationis prodromus*, he laid out what are now taught worldwide as **Steno's Laws**: that sedimentary layers are deposited horizontally, that they extend continuously until interrupted by some edge or barrier, and that any given layer formed after the one beneath it and before the one above. These three principles, superposition, original horizontality, and lateral continuity, gave geologists their first reliable method for reading rock strata as a timeline, centuries before radiometric dating existed.

Steno's path then turned sharply. He converted from Lutheranism to Catholicism in 1667, was ordained a priest in 1675, and became a reform-minded auxiliary bishop serving northern Germany, where he died in poverty in Schwerin in 1686. **Pope John Paul II** beatified him in 1988, recognizing a pastoral life as austere as his science had been precise.

Denmark remembers him institutionally rather than devotionally: the **Steno Museum** in Aarhus covers the history of science and medicine, while the **Steno Diabetes Center** in Copenhagen, a major clinical and research institution, carries his name as a tribute to a Dane who insisted that careful, physical observation, of a duct, a tooth, or a layer of stone, could rewrite how humanity understood time itself.
