Walk into almost any Danish living room, café, or ministry meeting room after dark, and you are unlikely to find a single harsh overhead bulb. Instead, light pools downward and sideways from layered shades, warm and shadowless. Much of that habit traces back to one object and one man: the PH lamp, designed by **Poul Henningsen** (1894–1967) and first shown at the 1925 Exposition des Arts Décoratifs in Paris, the same fair that gave Art Deco its name.

Henningsen trained as an architect but never finished his degree; he made his name instead as a journalist, satirist, and revue songwriter, sharply critical of pretension in Danish cultural life. His lighting work grew from a simpler complaint: electric bulbs, newly common in homes, were unbearably glaring. Earlier gas lamps had diffused their flame through mantles and glass; the bare filament bulb, by contrast, assaulted the eye directly. Henningsen set out to solve this mathematically rather than decoratively. His design used three nested, carefully calculated shades, an upper reflector, a middle diffuser, and a lower bowl, each curved so that no ray from the bulb reached the eye directly while the light itself was redistributed evenly across a room. The result, later refined into the famous **PH5** model launched in 1958 for the manufacturer **Louis Poulsen**'s fiftieth anniversary, became one of the most recognizable lamps in design history and remains in production today, still largely handmade in Denmark.

The PH lamp's success was not merely aesthetic; it crystallized something already stirring in Danish taste, a deep aversion to glare and a preference for soft, layered, indirect illumination over bright, uniform overhead light. That sensibility now sits comfortably alongside the modern Danish idea of hygge: dim, warm interiors are not a design affectation but, by Henningsen's logic, a rejection of visual discomfort. Danish building codes and interior conventions still favor low, warm-toned lighting over the brighter fixtures common elsewhere in Europe and North America.

Henningsen remained a combative public figure throughout his life, fleeing to Sweden during the German occupation after mocking the Nazis in print, and continuing to write and provoke after returning to Denmark. He died in 1967, but his lamps, still glowing without glare in homes from Copenhagen to abroad, remain his most lasting argument.
