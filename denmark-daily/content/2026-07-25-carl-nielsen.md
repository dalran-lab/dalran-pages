If Denmark has a musical soul, it was written by **Carl Nielsen** (1865–1931). Born into rural poverty on the island of **Funen (Fyn)** — the same island that produced H.C. Andersen — he was one of twelve children of a house-painter who moonlighted as a village fiddler. The boy's talent carried him from a military band in Odense to the **Royal Danish Academy of Music** in Copenhagen and, eventually, to the front rank of European composers.

Nielsen's reputation rests above all on his **six symphonies** — restless, surprising works that helped drag the form into the 20th century. The most beloved is the Fourth (1916), subtitled **"The Inextinguishable"** (*Det Uudslukkelige*), a hymn to the sheer will of life, famous for a duel between two sets of timpani. The Fifth is a stark drama of order versus chaos, complete with a side-drummer instructed to improvise as if trying to derail the orchestra.

But Danes love him for something more intimate. His opera **"Maskarade"** (1906) is treated as an unofficial national opera, and he wrote dozens of **songs and hymns** that ordinary Danes still sing together from the *Højskolesangbogen*, the folk high-school songbook. This tradition of communal singing is central to Danish culture — and Nielsen is its patron saint. His wife, **Anne Marie Carl-Nielsen**, was a distinguished sculptor whose equestrian statue of King Christian IX stands at Amalienborg.

For years his portrait looked out from the **100-krone banknote**, a rare honour for a composer. Today the **Carl Nielsen International Competition** in Odense draws young violinists, flautists and clarinettists from around the world, and his melodies remain so woven into daily life that most Danes can sing one without realising they are quoting their greatest composer.
