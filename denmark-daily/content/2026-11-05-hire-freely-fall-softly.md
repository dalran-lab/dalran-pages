Denmark lets employers hire and fire with a speed that would alarm most of continental Europe, yet its workers rank among the most secure and satisfied in the world. The paradox has a name: flexicurity, a term popularized in the 1990s to describe a Danish compromise stretching back to the 1899 "September Compromise" between the employer federation **Dansk Arbejdsgiverforening** and the trade union confederation **LO**, which traded employers' right to manage and dismiss for unions' right to organize and bargain collectively. No statutory minimum wage exists in Denmark; pay and conditions are set almost entirely through collective agreements, a system that has survived more than a century with periodic renegotiation.

The modern flexicurity triangle rests on three legs. First, flexible hiring and firing rules mean a Danish company can downsize in weeks, not years, without the severance litigation common in France or Germany. Second, a generous welfare safety net, administered through voluntary unemployment insurance funds called A-kasser, replaces a large share of lost income, historically among the highest replacement rates in the OECD, though reforms since the 2010s under governments including that of **Lars Løkke Rasmussen** have tightened eligibility periods and benefit duration. Third, and most distinctively, active labor market policy obliges the unemployed to engage in retraining, job search, and placement programs run through the state employment system, so that security is paired with an expectation of re-entry rather than passive support.

The architect credited with coining and championing the term in policy circles was the Danish Ministry of Employment under finance minister **Mogens Lykketoft** in the 1990s, as Denmark emerged from a period of high unemployment into what economists dubbed the "Danish job miracle." The European Commission later adopted flexicurity as a template for member states, though few have replicated it, partly because it depends on deep trust between unions and employers and on a tax-funded welfare state that assumes high taxation is politically acceptable.

For a newcomer to Copenhagen's labor market, the system explains much everyday behavior: workers change jobs more readily than elsewhere in Europe, employers recruit without the fear of permanent commitment, and job loss, while still stressful, rarely means catastrophe. It is a quietly radical bet that security need not come from job protection at all, but from the state and the market sharing the risk.
