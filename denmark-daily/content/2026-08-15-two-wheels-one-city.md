On an ordinary weekday, well over half of Copenhageners commuting to work or school do so by bicycle, a habit so deeply woven into the city's rhythm that rush hour is measured in the flow of cyclists across bridges rather than cars in lanes. This did not happen by accident. Organized cycling advocacy in Denmark dates to 1905, when **Cyklistforbundet**, the Danish Cyclists' Federation, was founded to defend riders' interests on roads increasingly shared with motor traffic. But the modern infrastructure that makes Copenhagen a global reference point took shape more recently, through deliberate, expensive municipal choices.

The clearest expression of this commitment is the **cykelsuperstier**, or cycle superhighway network, a project coordinated by the Capital Region of Denmark together with dozens of surrounding municipalities. The first route, the roughly 17.5-kilometer **Albertslundruten**, opened in April 2012, linking the suburb of Albertslund to central Copenhagen with a continuous, well-lit, priority-signaled path designed to make longer commutes by bike genuinely competitive with driving. Since then the network has grown into dozens of routes stretching hundreds of kilometers, engineered with features like footrests at intersections and pumps for flat tires, treating the bicycle commuter with the same seriousness once reserved for motorists.

Within the city itself, striking pieces of bridge architecture have become landmarks in their own right. The **Cykelslangen**, or Cycle Snake, an elevated orange ribbon designed by the firm **Dissing+Weitling** and opened in 2014, carries cyclists over Copenhagen's harbor near Fisketorvet, sparing them a set of stairs and connecting seamlessly to Islands Brygge. Two years later, the **Inderhavnsbroen**, or Inner Harbour Bridge, opened between Nyhavn and Christianshavn, giving pedestrians and cyclists a direct route across water previously requiring a long detour. Even the cargo bike, now ubiquitous for ferrying children and groceries, traces its lineage to Christiania, the freetown where residents **Lars Engstrøm** and **Amund Bjørklund** built the first three-wheeled Christiania bike in 1984. Together these threads show cycling in Copenhagen not as nostalgia but as sustained civic engineering.
