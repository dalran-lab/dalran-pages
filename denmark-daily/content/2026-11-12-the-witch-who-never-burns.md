Every year on the evening of 23 June, beaches and harbors across Denmark fill with families gathered around bonfires for **Sankt Hans Aften**, the eve of the feast of Saint John the Baptist. The date sits almost exactly on the summer solstice, and the fires themselves are older than the Christian calendar they now mark: Midsummer blazes were lit across pre-Christian Scandinavia to protect livestock, mark the turning of the sun, and ward off evil spirits believed to roam freely on the shortest night. When the medieval church absorbed the solstice into its own liturgy, it attached the feast to John the Baptist, whose birth the Gospel of Luke places six months before Christ's, and the bonfires survived the conversion largely intact.

The custom that gives the night its modern, slightly uncanny character, burning an effigy of a witch atop the pyre, is younger and darker than the solstice fires themselves. It echoes Denmark's own witch trials of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when hundreds of people, mostly women, were burned at the stake under laws against sorcery, with the island of Hven and the town of Køge among the grimmer sites of prosecution. By the nineteenth century the burning had become symbolic folklore rather than memory of persecution, reframed in popular tradition as sending the witch flying off to the Blocksberg, the Brocken mountain in Germany's Harz range, supposedly the gathering place of witches on Walpurgis Night.

The soundtrack to this is almost always the same: **Holger Drachmann's** "Vi elsker vort land" (We Love Our Country), written in 1885 and set to music by Peter Erasmus Lange-Müller, now universally known simply as the **Midsommervisen**. Drachmann, a Romantic poet and painter closely tied to the light and fishing community of **Skagen** at Denmark's northern tip, captured a national mood of landscape and belonging that made the song a fixture of the holiday within a generation.

Modern Sankt Hans Aften, organized today by local municipalities, sailing clubs and beach communities, blends all three layers at once: solstice fire, folk memory of persecution turned into harmless spectacle, and a Golden Age poet's hymn to Danish land and sea, sung by crowds who rarely stop to ask how old, or how dark, the tradition beneath their feet actually is.
