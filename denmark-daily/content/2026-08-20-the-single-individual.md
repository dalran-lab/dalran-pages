**Søren Aabye Kierkegaard** was born in Copenhagen on May 5, 1813, the youngest son of a wealthy, devout wool merchant, **Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard**, whose private guilt and melancholy over a blasphemous curse he had uttered as a shepherd boy haunted the household and, by Søren's own account, shaped his son's brooding temperament. Søren studied theology and philosophy at the University of Copenhagen, drifting for years before settling into a life devoted entirely to writing. In 1840 he became engaged to **Regine Olsen**, a decision he reversed within a year, breaking off the match in 1841 in an act of renunciation that echoes through his work as the paradigm of an impossible choice between the ethical demands of ordinary life and a higher, solitary calling.

Between 1843 and 1846 Kierkegaard produced, at extraordinary speed, the books that made him the philosopher historians now call the father of existentialism: *Either/Or*, *Fear and Trembling*, *The Concept of Anxiety*, and *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, most published under invented authors such as Johannes de Silentio and Johannes Climacus, so that each pseudonym could argue a worldview, aesthetic, ethical, or religious, from the inside rather than as Kierkegaard's own doctrine. He gave philosophy a vocabulary it still uses: angst, the leap of faith, and above all "the single individual" (*den Enkelte*), his insistence that truth is subjective and that no system, least of all Hegel's, could substitute for a person's own existential choice.

In his final years Kierkegaard turned that same intensity against the Church of Denmark itself, publishing a series of pamphlets called *The Moment* that accused the state church, and its leading bishop, **Hans Lassen Martensen**, of domesticating Christianity into comfortable "Christendom." He collapsed in the street in October 1855 and died at Frederiks Hospital on November 11, aged 42. He is buried at **Assistens Cemetery** in Nørrebro, a short walk from the lakes he circled daily while composing his books in his head, and his manuscripts are held today at the Royal Danish Library, whose Søren Kierkegaard Research Centre at the University of Copenhagen continues to edit and study a body of work that would later shape Sartre, Heidegger, and Camus.
