In 1572, a young Danish nobleman named **Tycho Brahe** observed a new star blazing in the constellation Cassiopeia, bright enough to be seen in daylight. It was, in fact, a supernova, though no one yet had that word. The sighting, and Brahe's exacting measurements proving the object lay far beyond the Moon, overturned the Aristotelian belief that the heavens were fixed and unchanging. It also made him Europe's most celebrated astronomer almost overnight, and it set him on a path that would make Denmark, briefly, the center of the observational universe.

King **Frederik II** rewarded Brahe with the island of Hven, in the Øresund strait between Zealand and Scania, a region then Danish and now Swedish. There, beginning in 1576, Brahe built **Uraniborg**, an extraordinary combine of palace, observatory and laboratory, named for Urania, the muse of astronomy. He later added a second, partly underground facility called **Stjerneborg** ("star castle"), designed to shield his instruments from wind. Working entirely before the telescope existed, Brahe achieved a precision in naked-eye observation that no one had matched, charting the positions of over 700 stars and tracking planetary motion, especially Mars, with unprecedented accuracy. He also proposed his own cosmological model, the Tychonic system, a compromise in which the Sun and Moon orbited the Earth while the other planets orbited the Sun, an attempt to reconcile the old geocentric order with the troubling elegance of Copernicus.

Brahe's fortunes soured after Frederik II's death. The young **Christian IV** and his court grew impatient with Brahe's wealth, his arrogance and his feuds with neighboring tenants. Stripped of his income from Hven, Brahe left Denmark in 1597 and eventually settled in Prague under the patronage of Holy Roman Emperor Rudolf II. There he hired a brilliant young assistant, **Johannes Kepler**, who after Brahe's death in 1601 used his meticulous decades of planetary data to derive the laws of elliptical orbits, the breakthrough Brahe himself never reached.

Little remains of Uraniborg today beyond earthworks and a reconstructed garden, but Brahe's legacy endures in Denmark's self-image as a nation of precise, patient observers, a thread running forward to Niels Bohr's institute and the country's enduring investment in exact science.
