In 1916, a carpenter named **Ole Kirk Christiansen** bought a small woodworking shop in the flat, unremarkable Jutland town of **Billund**. He made furniture and household goods, and only turned to toys in 1932, a year forced on him by circumstance: the Great Depression had crushed demand for his other work, and his wife Kristine had just died, leaving him to raise four sons. Wooden ducks, trucks and yo-yos kept the business alive. In 1934 he named the company **LEGO**, a contraction of the Danish phrase *leg godt*, "play well." It was later noticed, with some satisfaction, that *lego* also means "I put together" in Latin, though that was coincidence rather than design.

The decisive leap came in 1947, when Ole Kirk bought Denmark's first plastic injection-moulding machine, betting on a material many still distrusted as cheap and impermanent. His son **Godtfred Kirk Christiansen** pushed the plastic bricks further, and in 1958 patented the stud-and-tube coupling system that let bricks interlock securely in almost infinite combinations, the same mechanism, essentially unchanged, used in every LEGO brick sold today. That patent, more than any marketing, is what turned a regional toymaker into a design phenomenon: a single system flexible enough to build a spaceship or a cathedral, precise enough that a brick from 1958 still clicks onto one made this year.

Billund itself grew up around the factory. What was farmland became a company town in the most literal sense, and in 1968 the family opened **Legoland Billund**, drawing tourists to a place with no other reason for a visitor to stop. The town later gained its own airport, largely to serve them. In 2017 the striking white-terraced **LEGO House**, designed by Danish architect **Bjarke Ingels**, opened nearby as a public "home of the brick," reinforcing Billund's identity as company town and pilgrimage site at once.

The LEGO Group remains privately held through the family's holding company, **KIRKBI**, run today by descendants of Ole Kirk, a rare case of a Danish industrial giant staying in family hands across four generations while becoming, by some measures, the world's most powerful toy brand.
