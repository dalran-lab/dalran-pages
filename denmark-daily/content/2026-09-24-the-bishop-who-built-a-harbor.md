In 1167, a Danish bishop named **Absalon** raised a stone castle on a small island in the Øresund strait, at a fishing village then known as Havn — "the harbor." That fortress, built to defend against Wendish pirates raiding from the southern Baltic coast, is the conventional founding moment of **Copenhagen**, and the island it stood on is the same ground beneath **Christiansborg Palace** today, seat of the Danish parliament, the Supreme Court, and the Royal Reception Rooms.

Absalon was no ordinary cleric. Born around 1128 into the powerful **Hvide** clan, he was raised alongside the future king **Valdemar I**, whom he served as close advisor and, later, as archbishop of Lund from 1178. He embodied a type common in twelfth-century Scandinavia: the warrior-bishop, equally at home leading naval campaigns against Slavic pirate strongholds on Rügen as blessing altars. The chronicler **Saxo Grammaticus**, author of the *Gesta Danorum*, was Absalon's protégé and likely wrote at his patron's urging, which is why Absalon appears in that history as something close to a national hero.

The harbor settlement he fortified grew quickly. Its name shifted to **København**, meaning "merchants' harbor," reflecting the trade in herring and grain that made it valuable long before it became a political capital. Herring, salted and exported across northern Europe, was the medieval North Sea's equivalent of oil, and control of a well-defended harbor on the sound between Zealand and Skåne was immensely lucrative. That same strategic chokepoint later made the city a target: it was sacked by the Hanseatic League in 1249 and by Swedish forces repeatedly across the following centuries, precisely because whoever held Copenhagen controlled traffic through the Øresund.

Absalon's original castle was demolished and rebuilt many times. Archaeological remains of his foundations are still visible beneath Christiansborg, accessible to visitors in the **Ruins under Christiansborg** exhibit, a rare chance to stand inside the literal footprint of the city's origin. A bronze equestrian statue of Absalon, sword raised, stands today in **Højbro Plads** in the city center, a short walk from the site he chose. The choice of that small, defensible island over the open mainland shaped Copenhagen's entire subsequent geography, channeling growth around a harbor that remains, nine centuries later, the organizing feature of the Danish capital.
