On June 15, 1219, according to a legend first written down more than two centuries later, a red banner bearing a white cross fell from the sky above the battlefield of Lyndanisse, near modern Tallinn in Estonia. King **Valdemar II Sejr** was leading a crusading army against pagan Estonian tribes, and his forces were faltering until, the story goes, the flag descended and rallied them to victory. The banner became known as the **Dannebrog**, and Denmark has flown some version of it ever since, a claim that earned it a Guinness World Records entry as the oldest continuously used national flag still in existence.

Historians treat the sky-fall story as pious embellishment rather than fact; the earliest surviving mention comes from the 15th-century chronicler Christiern Pedersen, two hundred years after the supposed event. What is documented is that a red-and-white cross banner was already associated with Danish royal and church authority by the 14th century, likely descended from crusader flags carried by Danish knights fighting in the Baltic. The white Scandinavian cross, offset toward the flagpole rather than centered, later became the template copied by **Norway**, **Sweden**, **Finland**, and **Iceland** when each designed its own national flag, making the Dannebrog the ancestor of an entire family of Nordic crosses.

For centuries the plain rectangular flag was reserved for the king and the state, while ordinary Danes were largely forbidden from flying it. The navy and merchant fleet used a distinct swallow-tailed version, the **splitflag** or Orlogsflag, still flown today by Danish warships and, by royal permission, certain institutions. Only after a series of 19th-century decrees, formalized in the 1830s under **Frederik VI**, did private citizens gain the right to raise the rectangular civilian flag over their own homes, a privilege still legally distinct from the naval pennant.

June 15 itself, **Valdemarsdag**, carries a second layer of meaning: it also marks the day in 1920 when Denmark celebrated the reunification of Southern Jutland after a plebiscite following the First World War, and Danes flew the Dannebrog from castles and farmhouses alike. Today the flag remains woven into daily life far beyond politics, appearing on birthday cakes, at christenings, and along Copenhagen's streets each June, a symbol whose disputed origin has never dented its emotional grip on the country.
